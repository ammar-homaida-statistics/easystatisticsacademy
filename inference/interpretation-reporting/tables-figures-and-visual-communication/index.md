<section>

  <div class="content-narrow">

    <h2>Why Visual Communication Matters</h2>

    <p>
      Human beings often recognize patterns more quickly through visuals than through numerical summaries.
    </p>

    <p>
      A carefully designed figure can reveal trends, differences, and relationships that may be difficult to see in a paragraph of text.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Main goal:</strong>

      <p>
        Use visualizations to improve understanding without distorting the underlying data.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Tables vs Figures</h2>

    <p>
      Tables and figures serve different purposes.
    </p>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>Format</th>
            <th>Best For</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>Table</td>
            <td>Exact numerical values</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Figure</td>
            <td>Patterns and comparisons</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
      Readers often use tables when they need precise numbers and figures when they need quick insight.
    </p>

    <h2>When to Use Tables</h2>

    <p>
      Tables are useful when readers may need to:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Look up exact values</li>

      <li>Compare multiple statistics</li>

      <li>Review detailed results</li>

      <li>Reference numerical summaries</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Tables emphasize precision rather than visual impact.
    </p>

    <h2>When to Use Figures</h2>

    <p>
      Figures are useful when readers need to:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Identify trends</li>

      <li>Compare groups visually</li>

      <li>Recognize relationships</li>

      <li>Detect unusual patterns</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Figures emphasize interpretation and communication.
    </p>

    <h2>Common Statistical Figures</h2>

    <p>
      Frequently used visualizations include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Bar charts</li>

      <li>Histograms</li>

      <li>Scatterplots</li>

      <li>Boxplots</li>

      <li>Line charts</li>

      <li>Error-bar plots</li>

    </ul>

    <h2>Bar Charts</h2>

    <p>
      Bar charts are useful for comparing categories.
    </p>

    <p>
      For example:
    </p>

    

    <p>
      Differences between groups are immediately visible.
    </p>

    <h2>Histograms</h2>

    <p>
      Histograms display distributions.
    </p>

    <p>
      They help reveal:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Shape</li>

      <li>Spread</li>

      <li>Skewness</li>

      <li>Potential outliers</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Histograms are particularly useful during exploratory data analysis.
    </p>

    <h2>Scatterplots</h2>

    <p>
      Scatterplots visualize relationships between two quantitative variables.
    </p>

    <p>
      They can reveal:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Positive relationships</li>

      <li>Negative relationships</li>

      <li>Nonlinear patterns</li>

      <li>Outliers</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Scatterplots are often used when studying correlation and regression.
    </p>

    <h2>Boxplots</h2>

    <p>
      Boxplots summarize distributions using:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Median</li>

      <li>Quartiles</li>

      <li>Spread</li>

      <li>Potential outliers</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      They are useful for comparing distributions across groups.
    </p>

    <h2>Line Charts</h2>

    <p>
      Line charts are commonly used for data measured over time.
    </p>

    <p>
      Examples include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Stock prices</li>

      <li>Population growth</li>

      <li>Sales trends</li>

      <li>Experimental measurements</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      They emphasize change and trend.
    </p>

    <h2>Showing Uncertainty</h2>

    <p>
      Statistical graphics should often display uncertainty as well as estimates.
    </p>

    <p>
      Common approaches include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Confidence intervals</li>

      <li>Error bars</li>

      <li>Prediction bands</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      These additions help readers understand precision.
    </p>

    <h2>Why Error Bars Matter</h2>

    <p>
      A graph showing only means may hide substantial uncertainty.
    </p>

    <p>
      Error bars remind readers that estimates are not exact.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Good practice:</strong>

      <p>
        Whenever possible, visualize both estimates and uncertainty.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Keep Visuals Simple</h2>

    <p>
      Effective figures communicate information efficiently.
    </p>

    <p>
      Avoid unnecessary:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Decorations</li>

      <li>Colors</li>

      <li>Labels</li>

      <li>Visual effects</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Simplicity often improves clarity.
    </p>

    <h2>Avoid Misleading Axes</h2>

    <p>
      Graphs can become misleading when axes are manipulated.
    </p>

    <p>
      For example:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Truncated scales</li>

      <li>Unequal intervals</li>

      <li>Distorted aspect ratios</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      These choices can exaggerate or hide differences.
    </p>

    <h2>The Importance of Labels</h2>

    <p>
      Every table and figure should clearly identify:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Variables</li>

      <li>Units</li>

      <li>Groups</li>

      <li>Scales</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Readers should not need to guess what a graph represents.
    </p>

    <h2>Titles and Captions</h2>

    <p>
      Good titles summarize the main purpose of a visual.
    </p>

    <p>
      Captions can provide:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Context</li>

      <li>Definitions</li>

      <li>Study details</li>

      <li>Interpretation guidance</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Together they improve accessibility.
    </p>

    <h2>Choosing the Right Visualization</h2>

    <p>
      Different questions require different visuals.
    </p>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>Goal</th>
            <th>Common Choice</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>Compare categories</td>
            <td>Bar chart</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Show a distribution</td>
            <td>Histogram</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Compare distributions</td>
            <td>Boxplot</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Show relationships</td>
            <td>Scatterplot</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Show trends over time</td>
            <td>Line chart</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <h2>Visuals Should Support the Narrative</h2>

    <p>
      Figures and tables should reinforce the main message of a report.
    </p>

    <p>
      They should not exist simply because software can produce them.
    </p>

    <p>
      Every visual should serve a purpose.
    </p>

    <h2>Accessibility Considerations</h2>

    <p>
      Effective communication also requires accessibility.
    </p>

    <p>
      Good practices include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Readable labels</li>

      <li>Clear contrast</li>

      <li>Descriptive captions</li>

      <li>Simple layouts</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Visualizations should remain understandable to a wide audience.
    </p>

    <h2>Common Mistakes</h2>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Avoid these pitfalls:</strong>

      <ul class="bullets">

        <li>Using the wrong chart type</li>

        <li>Overloading visuals with information</li>

        <li>Omitting uncertainty measures</li>

        <li>Using misleading scales</li>

        <li>Providing poor labels or captions</li>

      </ul>

    </div>

    <h2>The Bigger Picture</h2>

    <p>
      Tables and figures are powerful communication tools.
    </p>

    <p>
      When designed thoughtfully, they make statistical findings easier to understand and evaluate.
    </p>

    <p>
      Effective visual communication complements numerical analysis and strengthens the overall quality of reporting.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Core message:</strong>

      <p>
        Good visual communication presents data honestly, clearly, and efficiently. Tables provide precision, figures reveal patterns, and both should help readers understand statistical evidence more effectively.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Looking Ahead</h2>

    <p>
      Statistical reporting also depends on transparency and reproducibility.
    </p>

    <p>
      Analysts should document methods, preserve workflows, and ensure that results can be verified and repeated.
    </p>

    <p>
      The next lesson introduces reproducibility principles and a practical checklist for reviewing statistical analyses before reporting conclusions.
    </p>

        <li>Tables provide precise numerical information</li>

        <li>Figures help reveal patterns and relationships</li>

        <li>Different visualization types serve different purposes</li>

        <li>Uncertainty should often be displayed alongside estimates</li>

        <li>Error bars and confidence intervals improve interpretation</li>

        <li>Clear labels and captions are essential</li>

        <li>Misleading scales can distort conclusions</li>

        <li>Effective visuals support accurate statistical communication</li>

      </ul>
