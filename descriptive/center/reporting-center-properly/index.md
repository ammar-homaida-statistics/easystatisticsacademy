    <h2>Why Reporting Matters</h2>

    <p>
      Statistics are used to support decisions,
      explain findings,
      and communicate information.
    </p>

    <p>
      A measure of center is useful only if readers understand what it represents.
    </p>

    <p>
      Good reporting transforms calculations into meaningful information.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Key idea:</strong>

      <p>
        A measure of center should be reported in a way that accurately reflects both the data and its context.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Always Name the Measure</h2>

    <p>
      The word
      <em>average</em>
      can be ambiguous.
    </p>

    <p>
      Readers may not know whether you mean:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Mean</li>

      <li>Median</li>

      <li>Mode</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Therefore,
      it is usually better to state the specific measure being reported.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Better: "The median household income was $58,000."
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Less clear: "The average household income was $58,000."
      </p>
    </div>

    <h2>Include Units</h2>

    <p>
      A measure of center without units often lacks meaning.
    </p>

    <p>
      Compare:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Mean = 72
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Mean exam score = 72 points
      </p>
    </div>

    <p>
      The second statement is much more informative.
    </p>

    <h2>Provide Context</h2>

    <p>
      Statistical summaries should always be connected to the variable being measured.
    </p>

    <p>
      Readers should know:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>What was measured</li>

      <li>Who or what was studied</li>

      <li>When the data were collected</li>

      <li>What the units represent</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Context transforms numbers into useful information.
    </p>

    <h2>Report an Appropriate Measure</h2>

    <p>
      Choosing the wrong measure can be misleading.
    </p>

    <p>
      For example,
      reporting the mean income of a highly skewed population may create a very different impression than reporting the median income.
    </p>

    <p>
      The measure should match the characteristics of the data.
    </p>

    <h2>Match the Measure to the Data Type</h2>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>

          <tr>
            <th>Data Type</th>
            <th>Common Measure(s)</th>
          </tr>

        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>Nominal</td>
            <td>Mode</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Ordinal</td>
            <td>Median, Mode</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Numerical</td>
            <td>Mean, Median, Mode</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
      The measurement scale determines which summaries are meaningful.
    </p>

    <h2>Report Center Together With Spread</h2>

    <p>
      A measure of center alone rarely tells the whole story.
    </p>

    <p>
      Consider these two datasets:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Dataset A: 48, 49, 50, 51, 52
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Dataset B: 10, 20, 50, 80, 90
      </p>
    </div>

    <p>
      Both have a center near 50,
      but their variability differs substantially.
    </p>

    <p>
      This is why center should eventually be reported together with measures of spread.
    </p>

    <h2>Avoid False Precision</h2>

    <p>
      Reporting excessive decimal places may imply more accuracy than actually exists.
    </p>

    <p>
      Example:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Mean age = 24.873649 years
      </p>
    </div>

    <p>
      In most contexts,
      reporting:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Mean age = 24.9 years
      </p>
    </div>

    <p>
      is more appropriate.
    </p>

    <h2>Be Careful With Outliers</h2>

    <p>
      When extreme values are present,
      readers should know how center was summarized.
    </p>

    <p>
      Possible approaches include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Reporting the median instead of the mean</li>

      <li>Reporting both mean and median</li>

      <li>Explaining the presence of outliers</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Transparency improves interpretation.
    </p>

    <h2>Report Sample Size</h2>

    <p>
      Measures of center become more meaningful when accompanied by the number of observations.
    </p>

    <p>
      Compare:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Mean score = 82
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Mean score = 82 (n = 2,500 students)
      </p>
    </div>

    <p>
      The second statement provides much more information.
    </p>

    <h2>Consider Reporting Multiple Measures</h2>

    <p>
      In many situations,
      reporting more than one measure of center can be useful.
    </p>

    <p>
      For example:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Mean income = $72,000<br>
        Median income = $58,000
      </p>
    </div>

    <p>
      The difference immediately suggests skewness.
    </p>

    <p>
      Multiple summaries often provide a richer understanding of the data.
    </p>

    <h2>Use Clear Language</h2>

    <p>
      Statistical communication should be understandable to the intended audience.
    </p>

    <p>
      Avoid unnecessary jargon when simpler language is sufficient.
    </p>

    <p>
      Clarity is one of the most important goals of statistical reporting.
    </p>

    <h2>Examples of Good Reporting</h2>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The median monthly rent for apartments in the city was $1,450."
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The mean exam score for the class was 78.4 points."
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The most common blood type in the sample was O-positive."
      </p>
    </div>

    <p>
      Each statement identifies the measure,
      the variable,
      and the context.
    </p>

    <h2>Common Reporting Mistakes</h2>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Using "average" without specifying the measure</li>

      <li>Ignoring units</li>

      <li>Reporting too many decimal places</li>

      <li>Using the mean for highly skewed data without explanation</li>

      <li>Failing to provide sample size</li>

      <li>Reporting center without context</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Avoiding these mistakes improves statistical communication.
    </p>

    <h2>The Role of Professional Judgment</h2>

    <p>
      Reporting statistics is not simply a mechanical process.
    </p>

    <p>
      Analysts must decide:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Which measure is most appropriate</li>

      <li>How much precision is needed</li>

      <li>What context should be provided</li>

      <li>What information readers need</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Good judgment is an important part of statistical practice.
    </p>

    <h2>Looking Ahead</h2>

    <p>
      Measures of center describe where a distribution is located.
    </p>

    <p>
      However,
      center alone cannot describe how spread out observations are.
    </p>

    <p>
      The next block introduces measures of spread,
      which quantify variability and dispersion within datasets.
    </p>

    <!-- TAKEAWAYS -->

    <div class="summary-box">

      <h2>Block 4 Takeaways</h2>

      <ul class="bullets">

        <li>Always identify the specific measure of center being reported</li>

        <li>Include units and context whenever possible</li>

        <li>Choose a measure that matches the data type and distribution</li>

        <li>Report sample size when appropriate</li>

        <li>Avoid false precision and unnecessary decimals</li>

        <li>Center should often be interpreted together with spread</li>

        <li>Clear communication is an essential part of statistical practice</li>

      </ul>
