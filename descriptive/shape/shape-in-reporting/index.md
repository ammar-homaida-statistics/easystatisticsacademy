    <h2>Why Shape Should Be Reported</h2>

    <p>
      Center and spread provide important summaries,
      but they do not tell the entire story.
    </p>

    <p>
      Two datasets may have identical means and standard deviations while exhibiting very different shapes.
    </p>

    <p>
      Reporting shape helps readers understand how observations are actually distributed.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Key idea:</strong>

      <p>
        Good statistical reporting describes center, spread, and shape together.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Shape Provides Context</h2>

    <p>
      Shape helps explain whether a reported average is representative of the data.
    </p>

    <p>
      For example,
      a mean may provide a useful summary in a symmetric distribution,
      but it may be misleading in a highly skewed distribution.
    </p>

    <p>
      Shape gives context to numerical summaries.
    </p>

    <h2>The Main Shape Features to Report</h2>

    <p>
      When describing a distribution,
      statisticians commonly consider:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Symmetry</li>

      <li>Skewness</li>

      <li>Modality</li>

      <li>Tails</li>

      <li>Outliers</li>

      <li>Clusters</li>

      <li>Gaps</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Not every report requires all of these features,
      but they provide a useful checklist.
    </p>

    <h2>Reporting Symmetry</h2>

    <p>
      If a distribution appears balanced,
      this should be stated clearly.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The distribution appears approximately symmetric around its center."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This statement immediately informs readers about the overall structure of the data.
    </p>

    <h2>Reporting Skewness</h2>

    <p>
      Skewness is one of the most commonly reported shape characteristics.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The distribution is moderately right-skewed."
      </p>

    </div>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The distribution exhibits a long left tail."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      These descriptions communicate information that averages alone cannot provide.
    </p>

    <h2>Reporting Outliers</h2>

    <p>
      Outliers should generally be acknowledged when present.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Several unusually large observations were identified in the upper tail."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This alerts readers to observations that may influence summary statistics.
    </p>

    <h2>Reporting Tails</h2>

    <p>
      Tail behavior is often important,
      particularly in risk-related analyses.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The distribution contains a long right tail, indicating occasional large values."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      Such descriptions help readers understand unusual outcomes and variability.
    </p>

    <h2>Reporting Multiple Peaks</h2>

    <p>
      Some distributions contain more than one peak.
    </p>

    <p>
      These distributions may indicate multiple subgroups within the data.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The histogram suggests two distinct peaks, indicating possible subpopulations."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This observation may lead to deeper investigation.
    </p>

    <h2>Use Visual Evidence</h2>

    <p>
      Shape is often best communicated through graphs.
    </p>

    <p>
      Common choices include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Histograms</li>

      <li>Density plots</li>

      <li>Boxplots</li>

      <li>Frequency polygons</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Visual displays often reveal patterns that are difficult to summarize numerically.
    </p>

    <h2>Combining Shape with Center and Spread</h2>

    <p>
      Effective reporting usually combines numerical summaries with shape descriptions.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Median income was $52,000 (IQR: $44,000–$71,000). The distribution was strongly right-skewed."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This statement provides center,
      spread,
      and shape simultaneously.
    </p>

    <h2>Choosing Language Carefully</h2>

    <p>
      Shape descriptions should be accurate and appropriately cautious.
    </p>

    <p>
      Terms such as:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Approximately symmetric</li>

      <li>Slightly skewed</li>

      <li>Moderately skewed</li>

      <li>Strongly skewed</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      are often more appropriate than overly precise claims.
    </p>

    <h2>Avoid Overinterpreting Shape</h2>

    <p>
      Small samples can produce irregular patterns purely by chance.
    </p>

    <p>
      Analysts should avoid making strong conclusions based solely on limited data.
    </p>

    <p>
      Shape descriptions should always be considered alongside sample size and context.
    </p>

    <h2>Shape and Statistical Decisions</h2>

    <p>
      Reporting shape is not merely descriptive.
    </p>

    <p>
      Shape influences:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Choice of center</li>

      <li>Choice of spread measure</li>

      <li>Outlier treatment</li>

      <li>Model selection</li>

      <li>Inferential procedures</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Readers benefit from understanding these characteristics.
    </p>

    <h2>Examples of Good Shape Reporting</h2>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Exam scores were approximately symmetric with no obvious outliers."
      </p>

    </div>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Household income exhibited substantial right skewness and several extreme observations."
      </p>

    </div>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The distribution appeared bimodal, suggesting two distinct groups."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      These statements are concise,
      informative,
      and easy to interpret.
    </p>

    <h2>Common Reporting Mistakes</h2>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Ignoring shape entirely</li>

      <li>Reporting only averages</li>

      <li>Failing to mention outliers</li>

      <li>Overstating conclusions from small samples</li>

      <li>Using technical terms without explanation</li>

      <li>Relying solely on numerical summaries</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Avoiding these mistakes improves communication and interpretation.
    </p>

    <h2>The Complete Distribution Description</h2>

    <p>
      A thorough description of a distribution often includes:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Center</li>

      <li>Spread</li>

      <li>Shape</li>

      <li>Outliers</li>

      <li>Sample size</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Together,
      these elements provide a comprehensive summary of the data.
    </p>
