    <h2>What Is the Empirical Rule?</h2>

    <p>
      The <strong>Empirical Rule</strong> is a guideline that describes how observations are distributed in many bell-shaped distributions.
    </p>

    <p>
      It explains how much of the data tends to fall within one,
      two,
      and three standard deviations of the mean.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Key idea:</strong>

      <p>
        For many bell-shaped distributions, most observations lie close to the mean and become increasingly rare as distance from the mean increases.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>The 68–95–99.7 Rule</h2>

    <p>
      The Empirical Rule is often called the
      <strong>68–95–99.7 Rule</strong>.
    </p>

    <p>
      It states that approximately:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>68% of observations fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean</li>

      <li>95% of observations fall within 2 standard deviations of the mean</li>

      <li>99.7% of observations fall within 3 standard deviations of the mean</li>

    </ul>

    <div class="example-box">

      <strong>Empirical Rule</strong>

      <p>
        68% → ±1 SD
      </p>

      <p>
        95% → ±2 SD
      </p>

      <p>
        99.7% → ±3 SD
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Why Does This Matter?</h2>

    <p>
      The rule provides an immediate sense of how unusual an observation may be.
    </p>

    <p>
      It allows us to judge whether a value is:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Typical</li>

      <li>Somewhat unusual</li>

      <li>Very unusual</li>

      <li>Extremely rare</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      without performing complex calculations.
    </p>

    <h2>A Bell-Shaped Distribution</h2>

    <p>
      The Empirical Rule applies to distributions that are approximately:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Symmetric</li>

      <li>Unimodal</li>

      <li>Bell-shaped</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      These distributions have a single central peak and gradually decreasing frequencies as observations move away from the center.
    </p>

    <h2>Visualizing the Rule</h2>

    <p>
      Imagine a bell-shaped distribution centered at the mean.
    </p>

    <p>
      Starting from the mean:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Most observations lie nearby</li>

      <li>Fewer observations lie farther away</li>

      <li>Very few observations lie extremely far away</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      This pattern creates the familiar bell shape.
    </p>

    <h2>An Example Using Exam Scores</h2>

    <p>
      Suppose exam scores have:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Mean = 70
      </p>

      <p>
        Standard Deviation = 10
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      Using the Empirical Rule:
    </p>

    <h3>Within One Standard Deviation</h3>

    <p>
      Scores between:
    </p>

    0

    <p>
      or:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        60 to 80
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      contain approximately 68% of students.
    </p>

    <h3>Within Two Standard Deviations</h3>

    <p>
      Scores between:
    </p>

    1

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        50 to 90
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      contain approximately 95% of students.
    </p>

    <h3>Within Three Standard Deviations</h3>

    <p>
      Scores between:
    </p>

    2

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        40 to 100
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      contain approximately 99.7% of students.
    </p>

    <h2>Interpreting Unusual Values</h2>

    <p>
      Observations far from the mean are uncommon.
    </p>

    <p>
      According to the Empirical Rule:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>About 32% lie beyond ±1 SD</li>

      <li>About 5% lie beyond ±2 SD</li>

      <li>About 0.3% lie beyond ±3 SD</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Values beyond three standard deviations are therefore extremely unusual.
    </p>

    <h2>Why Three Standard Deviations Are Important</h2>

    <p>
      Observations beyond three standard deviations often attract special attention.
    </p>

    <p>
      They may indicate:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Rare events</li>

      <li>Potential outliers</li>

      <li>Measurement errors</li>

      <li>Exceptional performance</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Analysts frequently investigate such observations more carefully.
    </p>

    <h2>The Empirical Rule and Shape</h2>

    <p>
      The rule works because bell-shaped distributions are symmetric.
    </p>

    <p>
      The percentages are distributed evenly around the mean.
    </p>

    <p>
      This connection reinforces the importance of understanding distribution shape.
    </p>

    <h2>When the Rule Does Not Apply</h2>

    <p>
      The Empirical Rule is not universal.
    </p>

    <p>
      It may perform poorly when distributions are:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Strongly skewed</li>

      <li>Multimodal</li>

      <li>Highly irregular</li>

      <li>Dominated by outliers</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Before applying the rule,
      analysts should examine the shape of the distribution.
    </p>

    <h2>Relationship to Standard Deviation</h2>

    <p>
      The Empirical Rule gives practical meaning to standard deviation.
    </p>

    <p>
      Instead of viewing standard deviation as merely a formula,
      we can interpret it as a measure of how observations are distributed around the mean.
    </p>

    <p>
      This makes standard deviation much more intuitive.
    </p>

    <h2>Applications of the Empirical Rule</h2>

    <p>
      The rule is widely used in:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Education</li>

      <li>Quality control</li>

      <li>Psychology</li>

      <li>Healthcare</li>

      <li>Manufacturing</li>

      <li>Research studies</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      It provides a quick approximation for understanding data distributions.
    </p>

    <h2>From Standard Deviations to Standardized Scores</h2>

    <p>
      The Empirical Rule relies on measuring distance from the mean in units of standard deviation.
    </p>

    <p>
      This idea leads naturally to one of the most important concepts in statistics:
      standardized scores.
    </p>

    <p>
      Standardized scores allow us to determine exactly how far an observation lies from the mean.
    </p>

    <h2>Why the Empirical Rule Matters</h2>

    <p>
      The Empirical Rule connects:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Shape</li>

      <li>Center</li>

      <li>Spread</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      into a single framework.
    </p>

    <p>
      It transforms standard deviation from a mathematical quantity into a practical tool for interpreting observations.
    </p>

    <h2>Looking Ahead</h2>

    <p>
      The Empirical Rule tells us how many observations tend to fall within certain distances of the mean.
    </p>

    <p>
      The next lesson introduces
      <strong>Z-scores</strong>,
      which provide a precise way to measure an individual observation's distance from the mean in standard deviation units.
    </p>
