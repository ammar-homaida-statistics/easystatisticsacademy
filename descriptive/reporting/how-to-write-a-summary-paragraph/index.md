    <h2>Why Summary Paragraphs Matter</h2>

    <p>
      Readers often do not have time to examine every table,
      graph,
      or statistic in a report.
    </p>

    <p>
      A summary paragraph provides the most important findings in a single,
      easy-to-read section.
    </p>

    <p>
      It helps readers quickly understand the main message of the analysis.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Key idea:</strong>

      <p>
        A summary paragraph should explain what the data show, not simply repeat numerical results.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>The Goal of a Summary Paragraph</h2>

    <p>
      A good summary paragraph answers the question:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "What should the reader remember about this dataset?"
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      Every sentence should contribute to that goal.
    </p>

    <h2>The Building Blocks</h2>

    <p>
      Most descriptive summary paragraphs include some combination of:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Context</li>

      <li>Center</li>

      <li>Spread</li>

      <li>Shape</li>

      <li>Outliers or unusual features</li>

      <li>Main interpretation</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      These elements come directly from the complete-description checklist.
    </p>

    <h2>Start with Context</h2>

    <p>
      Readers should immediately know what data are being discussed.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Exam scores from 250 university students were analyzed."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This opening establishes the subject of the analysis.
    </p>

    <h2>Describe the Center</h2>

    <p>
      After introducing the data,
      report where observations tend to be located.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Scores were centered around 78 points."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      The exact measure of center may be included when appropriate.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The mean score was 78.4 points."
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Describe the Spread</h2>

    <p>
      Readers should understand how much variability exists.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Variability was moderate, with a standard deviation of 8.1 points."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      Spread provides context for interpreting the center.
    </p>

    <h2>Describe the Shape</h2>

    <p>
      Shape often provides important information that averages alone cannot reveal.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The distribution appeared approximately symmetric."
      </p>

    </div>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The distribution was moderately right-skewed."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      The description should match the observed shape.
    </p>

    <h2>Mention Important Unusual Features</h2>

    <p>
      If outliers,
      clusters,
      or gaps are present,
      they should usually be mentioned.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Two unusually large observations were identified in the upper tail."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      Important features should not be hidden from readers.
    </p>

    <h2>Finish with Interpretation</h2>

    <p>
      The final sentence often explains what the findings mean.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Overall, student performance was generally strong and relatively consistent."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This converts statistics into a practical message.
    </p>

    <h2>A Simple Template</h2>

    <p>
      Many descriptive reports can be written using a straightforward structure:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Context → Center → Spread → Shape → Unusual Features → Interpretation
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This structure works well for many introductory analyses.
    </p>

    <h2>An Incomplete Example</h2>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The mean score was 78.4 and the standard deviation was 8.1."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This statement reports statistics,
      but provides little interpretation.
    </p>

    <h2>A Better Example</h2>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Exam scores from 250 students were centered around 78 points and showed moderate variability. The distribution appeared approximately symmetric with no obvious outliers. Overall, student performance was relatively consistent across the sample."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This version communicates far more information.
    </p>

    <h2>Writing for Different Audiences</h2>

    <p>
      The level of detail should depend on the audience.
    </p>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>

          <tr>
            <th>Audience</th>
            <th>Preferred Style</th>
          </tr>

        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>General Public</td>
            <td>Simple language and interpretation</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Managers</td>
            <td>Results and implications</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Researchers</td>
            <td>More statistical detail</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Technical Analysts</td>
            <td>Detailed summaries and metrics</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
      Good communication adapts to the reader.
    </p>

    <h2>Balance Numbers and Interpretation</h2>

    <p>
      A strong summary paragraph combines numerical evidence with explanation.
    </p>

    <p>
      Too many numbers can overwhelm readers.
    </p>

    <p>
      Too little evidence can weaken conclusions.
    </p>

    <p>
      Effective reporting balances both.
    </p>

    <h2>Common Writing Mistakes</h2>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Listing statistics without interpretation</li>

      <li>Ignoring shape</li>

      <li>Ignoring outliers</li>

      <li>Using excessive technical jargon</li>

      <li>Repeating every value from a table</li>

      <li>Failing to explain why results matter</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Avoiding these mistakes improves readability.
    </p>

    <h2>Use Statistics to Support the Story</h2>

    <p>
      The purpose of a summary paragraph is not to showcase calculations.
    </p>

    <p>
      The purpose is to explain what the data reveal.
    </p>

    <p>
      Statistics should support the story rather than replace it.
    </p>

    <h2>A Full Example</h2>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Household income data from 1,200 households were analyzed. The median income was $52,000, and variability was substantial. The distribution was strongly right-skewed, with several unusually high incomes creating a long upper tail. Overall, most households earned moderate incomes, while a relatively small number earned substantially more than the majority."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This example combines context,
      center,
      spread,
      shape,
      unusual features,
      and interpretation into a single coherent paragraph.
    </p>

    <h2>Summary Paragraphs as a Professional Skill</h2>

    <p>
      Writing clear statistical summaries is an essential professional skill.
    </p>

    <p>
      Analysts who can explain findings effectively often have greater impact than those who only produce calculations.
    </p>

    <p>
      Communication transforms analysis into action.
    </p>

    <h2>Looking Ahead</h2>

    <p>
      Summary paragraphs communicate overall findings,
      but tables remain one of the most important tools for presenting detailed statistical information.
    </p>

    <p>
      The next lesson explores how to design,
      organize,
      and report tables professionally.
    </p>
