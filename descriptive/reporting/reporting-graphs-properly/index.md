      A well-designed graph can reveal patterns immediately,
      while a poorly designed graph can confuse readers or even create misleading conclusions.

  <div class="content-narrow">

    <h2>Why Graphs Matter</h2>

    <p>
      Human beings are often better at recognizing visual patterns than numerical patterns.
    </p>

    <p>
      A graph can reveal trends,
      differences,
      relationships,
      clusters,
      and unusual observations much faster than a table of numbers.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Key idea:</strong>

      <p>
        Graphs help readers see patterns. Tables help readers see exact values.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>The Purpose of a Graph</h2>

    <p>
      Every graph should answer a question or communicate a specific message.
    </p>

    <p>
      A graph is not decoration.
    </p>

    <p>
      It is a communication tool.
    </p>

    <p>
      Before creating a graph, ask:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>What pattern should readers notice?</li>

      <li>What comparison should readers make?</li>

      <li>What conclusion should the graph support?</li>

    </ul>

    <h2>Every Graph Needs a Title</h2>

    <p>
      Readers should immediately understand what the graph displays.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Poor title:
      </p>

      <p>
        Results
      </p>

    </div>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Better title:
      </p>

      <p>
        Distribution of Final Exam Scores
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      Clear titles improve interpretation.
    </p>

    <h2>Always Label Axes</h2>

    <p>
      Axis labels are essential.
    </p>

    <p>
      Without labels,
      readers cannot determine what the graph measures.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        X-axis: Age (Years)
      </p>

      <p>
        Y-axis: Frequency
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      Labels should include units whenever applicable.
    </p>

    <h2>Choose the Correct Graph Type</h2>

    <p>
      Different graphs are designed for different types of data.
    </p>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>

          <tr>
            <th>Goal</th>
            <th>Recommended Graph</th>
          </tr>

        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>Compare categories</td>
            <td>Bar Chart</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Show proportions</td>
            <td>Pie Chart</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Show distribution</td>
            <td>Histogram</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Show relationship</td>
            <td>Scatterplot</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Show change over time</td>
            <td>Line Chart</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
      Using the wrong graph can hide important information.
    </p>

    <h2>Keep Graphs Simple</h2>

    <p>
      Simplicity improves understanding.
    </p>

    <p>
      Avoid unnecessary:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Colors</li>

      <li>Patterns</li>

      <li>3D effects</li>

      <li>Decorative elements</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Readers should focus on the data,
      not on graphic design features.
    </p>

    <h2>Avoid Chartjunk</h2>

    <p>
      The term <strong>chartjunk</strong> refers to visual elements that do not improve understanding.
    </p>

    <p>
      Examples include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Excessive shading</li>

      <li>Unnecessary images</li>

      <li>Decorative backgrounds</li>

      <li>Complex 3D effects</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Good graphs emphasize information rather than decoration.
    </p>

    <h2>Do Not Distort Axes</h2>

    <p>
      One of the most common visualization mistakes involves manipulating axis scales.
    </p>

    <p>
      Truncated axes can exaggerate small differences.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        A change from 95 to 100 may appear dramatic if the axis begins at 90 instead of 0.
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      Readers should be able to trust the graph's visual representation.
    </p>

    <h2>Use Appropriate Scales</h2>

    <p>
      Axis scales should:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Be easy to read</li>

      <li>Use sensible intervals</li>

      <li>Reflect the actual data range</li>

      <li>Avoid unnecessary distortion</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Appropriate scaling improves interpretation.
    </p>

    <h2>Display Enough Information</h2>

    <p>
      Graphs should provide sufficient context.
    </p>

    <p>
      Important elements may include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Titles</li>

      <li>Axis labels</li>

      <li>Units</li>

      <li>Legends</li>

      <li>Data sources</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Missing information can make graphs difficult to interpret.
    </p>

    <h2>Use Legends Carefully</h2>

    <p>
      When multiple groups are displayed,
      legends help readers identify categories.
    </p>

    <p>
      However,
      unnecessary legends should be avoided.
    </p>

    <p>
      If labels can be placed directly on the graph,
      that is often preferable.
    </p>

    <h2>Graphs Should Support the Message</h2>

    <p>
      Every graph should reinforce the main findings of the analysis.
    </p>

    <p>
      Readers should be able to identify the important pattern quickly.
    </p>

    <p>
      If a graph does not contribute to understanding,
      it may not be necessary.
    </p>

    <h2>Interpret the Graph</h2>

    <p>
      A graph should rarely be presented without explanation.
    </p>

    <p>
      Readers benefit from a short interpretation.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The histogram suggests a moderately right-skewed distribution."
      </p>

    </div>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The scatterplot indicates a positive relationship between study time and exam performance."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      Interpretation helps readers focus on the important features.
    </p>

    <h2>Example of Effective Communication</h2>

    <p>
      Consider a histogram of exam scores.
    </p>

    <p>
      A weak report might simply display the graph.
    </p>

    <p>
      A stronger report would include:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Exam scores were concentrated between 70 and 90 points. The distribution appeared approximately symmetric, with no obvious outliers."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      The graph and interpretation work together.
    </p>

    <h2>Common Graph Mistakes</h2>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Missing titles</li>

      <li>Missing axis labels</li>

      <li>Missing units</li>

      <li>Using the wrong graph type</li>

      <li>Excessive decoration</li>

      <li>Misleading scales</li>

      <li>No interpretation</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Avoiding these mistakes improves clarity and trustworthiness.
    </p>

    <h2>Graphs and Ethics</h2>

    <p>
      Graphs can strongly influence perception.
    </p>

    <p>
      Analysts therefore have a responsibility to present data honestly.
    </p>

    <p>
      Visualization should clarify reality rather than manipulate it.
    </p>

    <h2>Tables and Graphs Together</h2>

    <p>
      Tables and graphs are complementary tools.
    </p>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>

          <tr>
            <th>Tool</th>
            <th>Best For</th>
          </tr>

        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>Table</td>
            <td>Exact values</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Graph</td>
            <td>Patterns and trends</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
      Effective reports often use both.
    </p>

    <h2>A Professional Graph Checklist</h2>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Clear title</li>

      <li>Correct graph type</li>

      <li>Axis labels included</li>

      <li>Units reported</li>

      <li>Appropriate scales</li>

      <li>Minimal chartjunk</li>

      <li>Supporting interpretation provided</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      This checklist can help ensure that graphs communicate effectively.
    </p>

    <h2>Why Graph Reporting Matters</h2>

    <p>
      Good graphs allow readers to understand data quickly and accurately.
    </p>

    <p>
      Poor graphs can create confusion,
      hide important information,
      or even lead to incorrect conclusions.
    </p>

    <p>
      Effective visualization is therefore a critical part of statistical communication.
    </p>

    <h2>Looking Ahead</h2>

    <p>
      Graphs often reveal relationships between variables.
    </p>

    <p>
      However,
      an observed relationship does not necessarily imply that one variable causes another.
    </p>

    <p>
      The next lesson introduces one of the most important principles in statistics:
      association does not imply causation.
    </p>

    <div class="summary-box">

      <h2>Lesson Takeaways</h2>

      <ul class="bullets">

        <li>Graphs help reveal patterns and trends quickly</li>

        <li>Every graph should have a clear purpose and title</li>

        <li>Axes should always be labeled and include units when appropriate</li>

        <li>The correct graph type depends on the analytical goal</li>

        <li>Misleading scales and excessive decoration should be avoided</li>

        <li>Graphs should be accompanied by interpretation</li>

        <li>Effective visualizations improve communication and decision-making</li>

      </ul>

    </div>
