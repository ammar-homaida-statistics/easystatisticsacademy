<section class="section">
  <div class="section-head">
    <h1>Association ≠ Causation</h1>
    <p class="lead">
      One of the most important rules in statistics:
      <strong>Descriptive statistics describe relationships — they do not prove cause.</strong>
    </p>
  </div>

  <hr>

  <h2>1. What Is Association?</h2>

  <p>
    Association means two variables move together.
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>Higher study time → higher grades</li>
    <li>Higher advertising spending → higher sales</li>
    <li>Higher temperature → higher ice cream sales</li>
  </ul>

  This describes a pattern — not a cause.

  <hr>

  <h2>2. What Is Causation?</h2>

  <p>
    Causation means one variable directly produces a change in another.
  </p>

  To claim causation, we need:
  <ul>
    <li>Controlled experimental design</li>
    <li>Randomization</li>
    <li>Elimination of confounding variables</li>
  </ul>

  Descriptive summaries alone cannot provide this.

  <hr>

  <h2>3. The Classic Example</h2>

  Ice cream sales and drowning incidents both increase in summer.

  Does ice cream cause drowning?

  No.

  The hidden variable:
  <strong>Temperature</strong>

  This is called a confounder.

  <hr>

  <h2>4. Why This Matters in Reporting</h2>

  Weak reporting:

  ❌ “More screen time causes lower academic performance.”

  Responsible reporting:

  ✔ “Screen time is negatively associated with academic performance.”

  The second statement is descriptive.
  The first claims causality without evidence.

  <hr>

  <h2>5. Correlation Does Not Imply Causation</h2>

  Even strong correlations can be:
  - Coincidental
  - Driven by third variables
  - Result of reverse causality

  Always ask:
  <ul>
    <li>Is there a third variable?</li>
    <li>Is direction clear?</li>
    <li>Is design experimental or observational?</li>
  </ul>

  <hr>

  <h2>6. Safe Language in Descriptive Reporting</h2>

  Use:
  - “Associated with”
  - “Related to”
  - “Observed relationship”
  - “Pattern suggests”

  Avoid:
  - “Leads to”
  - “Causes”
  - “Results in”
  - “Determines”

  Unless experimental evidence exists.

  <hr>

  <h2>7. Graphs Can Mislead</h2>

  Scatterplots show relationship strength.
  They do not prove direction or cause.

  Interpretation must match design.

  <hr>

  <h2>Summary</h2>

  <ul>
    <li>Association describes patterns.</li>
    <li>Causation requires experimental control.</li>
    <li>Descriptive statistics do not prove cause.</li>
    <li>Use careful, neutral language.</li>
  </ul>
