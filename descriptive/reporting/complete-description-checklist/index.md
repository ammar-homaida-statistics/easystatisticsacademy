    <h1>Complete Description Checklist</h1>

      One of the most common mistakes in descriptive statistics is reporting only part of the story.
    </p>

    <p class="lead">

    <h2>Why Use a Checklist?</h2>

    <p>
      Analysts often focus on a few statistics and overlook important details.
    </p>

    <p>
      A checklist helps ensure that every major aspect of a distribution has been considered.
    </p>

    <p>
      It also improves consistency across reports and analyses.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Key idea:</strong>

      <p>
        A good statistical description should be systematic rather than accidental.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>The Goal of Description</h2>

    <p>
      The purpose of descriptive statistics is to answer a simple question:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "What does this dataset look like?"
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      A complete description should allow someone unfamiliar with the data to form a reasonably accurate picture of the distribution.
    </p>

    <h2>The Five-Part Checklist</h2>

    <p>
      For most numerical datasets,
      a complete description should address:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Context</li>

      <li>Center</li>

      <li>Spread</li>

      <li>Shape</li>

      <li>Unusual Features</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Together,
      these components provide a strong foundation for reporting.
    </p>

    <h2>Context Comes First</h2>

    <p>
      Before discussing statistics,
      readers need to know what is being measured.
    </p>

    <p>
      Context includes:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>The variable being studied</li>

      <li>The population or sample</li>

      <li>The measurement units</li>

      <li>The purpose of the analysis</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Without context,
      statistical summaries have little meaning.
    </p>

    <h2>Describe the Center</h2>

    <p>
      Center tells readers where observations tend to be located.
    </p>

    <p>
      Common measures include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Mean</li>

      <li>Median</li>

      <li>Mode</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      The choice should match the characteristics of the data.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The median household income was $52,000."
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Describe the Spread</h2>

    <p>
      Spread tells readers how much variability exists.
    </p>

    <p>
      Common measures include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Range</li>

      <li>Interquartile Range</li>

      <li>Variance</li>

      <li>Standard Deviation</li>

    </ul>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The standard deviation was 8.1 points."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      Center without spread is usually incomplete.
    </p>

    <h2>Describe the Shape</h2>

    <p>
      Shape explains how observations are distributed around the center.
    </p>

    <p>
      Important questions include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Is the distribution symmetric?</li>

      <li>Is it skewed?</li>

      <li>Does it have multiple peaks?</li>

      <li>Are the tails unusually long?</li>

    </ul>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The distribution was moderately right-skewed."
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Identify Unusual Features</h2>

    <p>
      Special features often deserve separate attention.
    </p>

    <p>
      Examples include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Outliers</li>

      <li>Clusters</li>

      <li>Gaps</li>

      <li>Extreme observations</li>

      <li>Possible subgroups</li>

    </ul>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Two unusually large observations appeared in the upper tail."
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>The Complete Description Formula</h2>

    <p>
      Many introductory statistics courses summarize descriptive reporting with a simple framework:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Context + Center + Spread + Shape + Unusual Features
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This formula provides a practical checklist for most descriptive analyses.
    </p>

    <h2>A Weak Description</h2>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "The mean score was 78."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This statement provides only one piece of information.
    </p>

    <p>
      Readers learn little about variability,
      shape,
      or unusual observations.
    </p>

    <h2>A Stronger Description</h2>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        "Exam scores were centered around 78 points, showed moderate variability, appeared approximately symmetric, and contained no obvious outliers."
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This statement provides a much more complete picture of the distribution.
    </p>

    <h2>Using Graphs with the Checklist</h2>

    <p>
      Graphs often help identify checklist components.
    </p>

    <p>
      For example:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Histograms reveal shape</li>

      <li>Boxplots reveal spread and outliers</li>

      <li>Scatterplots reveal unusual observations</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Visualizations and statistical summaries should be used together.
    </p>

    <h2>Adjusting for Different Data Types</h2>

    <p>
      Not every dataset requires every checklist component.
    </p>

    <p>
      For categorical variables,
      center and spread may not be meaningful.
    </p>

    <p>
      Instead,
      emphasis may be placed on:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Frequencies</li>

      <li>Percentages</li>

      <li>Dominant categories</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      The checklist should be adapted to the type of data being analyzed.
    </p>

    <h2>Common Omissions</h2>

    <p>
      Analysts frequently forget to report:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Units</li>

      <li>Sample size</li>

      <li>Outliers</li>

      <li>Shape</li>

      <li>Context</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      These omissions can reduce the usefulness of a report.
    </p>

    <h2>Sample Size Matters</h2>

    <p>
      Although not formally part of the checklist,
      sample size often provides important context.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Mean = 82.1, SD = 7.5, n = 1,250
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      This summary is generally more informative than reporting the statistics alone.
    </p>

    <h2>A Reporting Workflow</h2>

    <p>
      A useful workflow is:
    </p>

    <ol>

      <li>Identify the variable and context.</li>

      <li>Choose an appropriate measure of center.</li>

      <li>Choose an appropriate measure of spread.</li>

      <li>Examine shape.</li>

      <li>Look for unusual features.</li>

      <li>Write a concise interpretation.</li>

    </ol>

    <p>
      Following this process reduces the chance of overlooking important information.
    </p>

    <h2>Why Completeness Matters</h2>

    <p>
      Incomplete descriptions can lead to incorrect conclusions.
    </p>

    <p>
      Complete descriptions improve:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Communication</li>

      <li>Interpretation</li>

      <li>Decision-making</li>

      <li>Reproducibility</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      A complete description gives readers confidence that important features have been considered.
    </p>

    <h2>The Checklist as a Habit</h2>

    <p>
      Experienced analysts often apply this framework automatically.
    </p>

    <p>
      Over time,
      the checklist becomes a habit rather than a formal procedure.
    </p>

    <p>
      Developing that habit is an important step toward statistical maturity.
    </p>

    <h2>Looking Ahead</h2>

    <p>
      Knowing what to include is only part of reporting.
    </p>

    <p>
      The next lesson shows how to combine these components into a clear,
      concise,
      and professional summary paragraph.
    </p>

      <h2>Lesson Takeaways</h2>

      <ul class="bullets">

        <li>A complete description should be systematic and comprehensive</li>

        <li>Context gives meaning to statistical summaries</li>

        <li>Center, spread, and shape should usually be reported together</li>

        <li>Outliers and unusual features should not be ignored</li>

        <li>The checklist helps prevent important omissions</li>

        <li>Graphs and statistics work best when used together</li>

        <li>Complete reporting improves interpretation and communication</li>
