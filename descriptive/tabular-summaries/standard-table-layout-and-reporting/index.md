    <h2>Why Table Design Matters</h2>

    <p>
      Two tables can contain exactly the same data
      but communicate very differently.
    </p>

    <p>
      Poorly designed tables:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">
      <li>Create confusion</li>
      <li>Hide important findings</li>
      <li>Increase interpretation errors</li>
      <li>Reduce professional credibility</li>
    </ul>

    <p>
      Good tables make results easy to understand.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Key idea:</strong>

      <p>
        A table should communicate information,
        not force readers to search for it.
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
  Every table should be designed around a specific question
  that the reader can answer quickly from the displayed information.
</p>

<div class="concept-box">

  <strong>Important insight:</strong>

  <p>
    A well-designed table reduces the effort required
    to understand the data.
  </p>

</div>

    <h2>The Basic Components of a Statistical Table</h2>

    <p>
      Most professional tables contain:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Table number (optional in simple reports)</li>

      <li>Clear title</li>

      <li>Column headings</li>

      <li>Row labels</li>

      <li>Data values</li>

      <li>Totals (when appropriate)</li>

      <li>Notes or footnotes (if needed)</li>

    </ul>

    <img src="{{ 'descriptive/images/table-components.png' | relative_url }}"
     alt="Components of a statistical table"
     style="margin-top:15px; max-width:100%; height:auto;">

<p>
Professional tables contain clearly labeled components that support interpretation.
</p>

    <p>
      Every component should contribute to understanding.
    </p>

    <h2>A Poorly Designed Table</h2>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>A</th>
            <th>B</th>
            <th>C</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>12</td>
            <td>45</td>
            <td>22</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>18</td>
            <td>40</td>
            <td>17</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
      Problems:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>No title</li>

      <li>No meaningful labels</li>

      <li>No context</li>

      <li>No explanation of units</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Readers cannot determine what the values represent.
    </p>

    <h2>A Better Table</h2>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <caption>
          Student Enrollment by Academic Program
        </caption>

        <thead>

          <tr>
            <th>Program</th>
            <th>Male</th>
            <th>Female</th>
            <th>Total</th>
          </tr>

        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>Statistics</td>
            <td>45</td>
            <td>55</td>
            <td>100</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Engineering</td>
            <td>80</td>
            <td>20</td>
            <td>100</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
  In professional reports, frequencies are often accompanied
  by percentages to improve interpretation.
</p>

    <p>
      The table now communicates clearly and efficiently.
    </p>

    <img src="{{ 'descriptive/images/poor-vs-good-table.png' | relative_url }}"
     alt="Poor versus good table design"
     style="margin-top:15px; max-width:100%; height:auto;">

<p>
Good table design improves clarity, context, and interpretation.
</p>

    <h2>Use Informative Titles</h2>

    <p>
      Table titles should describe:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>What is being measured</li>

      <li>Who or what was studied</li>

      <li>The scope of the data</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Weak title:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">
      <p>Results Table</p>
    </div>

    <p>
      Strong title:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">
      <p>Distribution of Blood Types Among Hospital Patients</p>
    </div>

    <h2>Label Rows and Columns Clearly</h2>

    <p>
      Every column and row should have meaningful labels.
    </p>

    <p>
      Avoid:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Abbreviations readers may not know</li>

      <li>Unexplained codes</li>

      <li>Ambiguous names</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Clear labels reduce interpretation errors.
    </p>

    <h2>Include Units When Necessary</h2>

    <p>
      Numerical values often require measurement units.
    </p>

    <p>
      Example:
    </p>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>

          <tr>
            <th>Participant</th>
            <th>Weight (kg)</th>
            <th>Height (cm)</th>
          </tr>

        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>P01</td>
            <td>68</td>
            <td>172</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>P02</td>
            <td>75</td>
            <td>180</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
      Units prevent ambiguity.
    </p>

    <h2>Align Numbers Consistently</h2>

    <p>
      Numerical columns should be aligned consistently.
    </p>

    <p>
      This improves:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Readability</li>

      <li>Comparison</li>

      <li>Professional appearance</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Most statistical software automatically applies proper alignment.
    </p>

    <p>
  Decimal places should also be reported consistently
  throughout a table.
</p>

    <h2>Include Totals When Useful</h2>

    <p>
      Totals often help readers understand distributions.
    </p>

    <p>
      Example:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Row totals</li>

      <li>Column totals</li>

      <li>Grand totals</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Totals make percentages easier to interpret.
    </p>

    <h2>Report Frequencies and Percentages Together</h2>

    <p>
      For categorical data,
      professional reports often present:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Frequency counts</li>

      <li>Percentages</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Example:
    </p>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>

          <tr>
            <th>Response</th>
            <th>Frequency</th>
            <th>Percent</th>
          </tr>

        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>Yes</td>
            <td>120</td>
            <td>60%</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>No</td>
            <td>80</td>
            <td>40%</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
      Showing both provides complete context.
    </p>

    <p>
  Frequencies show how many observations occurred,
  while percentages show how large those counts are relative to the total.
</p>

    <h2>Handle Missing Values Transparently</h2>

    <p>
      Missing observations should usually be reported.
    </p>

    <p>
      Example:
    </p>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>

          <tr>
            <th>Response</th>
            <th>Frequency</th>
          </tr>

        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>Yes</td>
            <td>120</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>No</td>
            <td>75</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Missing</td>
            <td>5</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
      Transparency improves reporting quality.
    </p>

    <h2>Use Footnotes When Needed</h2>

    <p>
      Footnotes help explain:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Special calculations</li>

      <li>Definitions</li>

      <li>Exceptions</li>

      <li>Data limitations</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Good footnotes reduce misunderstandings.
    </p>

    <h2>Tables Should Be Self-Contained</h2>

    <p>
      Ideally,
      readers should understand a table
      without searching elsewhere for explanations.
    </p>

    <p>
      A good table contains:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Meaningful title</li>

      <li>Clear labels</li>

      <li>Units</li>

      <li>Totals</li>

      <li>Relevant notes</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Self-contained tables improve communication.
    </p>

    <h2>Professional Reporting Standards</h2>

    <p>
      Whether using:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>SPSS</li>

      <li>R</li>

      <li>Python</li>

      <li>Excel</li>

      <li>SAS</li>

      <li>Stata</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      the same principles apply:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Clarity</li>

      <li>Consistency</li>

      <li>Accuracy</li>

      <li>Transparency</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Good reporting is an essential part of good statistics.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

  <strong>Key insight:</strong>

  <p>
    Good statistical tables do more than present numbers —
    they make results understandable, transparent,
    and easy to interpret.
  </p>

</div>

<h2>Optional: Tables in SPSS Output</h2>

<ul class="bullets">
  <li>SPSS automatically generates formatted statistical tables</li>
  <li>Titles and labels should still be reviewed and edited when necessary</li>
  <li>Professional reports often refine SPSS output before publication</li>
</ul>

    <!-- TAKEAWAYS -->
    <div class="summary-box">

      <h2>Lesson Takeaways</h2>

      <ul class="bullets">

        <li>Well-designed tables improve communication and interpretation</li>

        <li>Every table should have a clear title and meaningful labels</li>

        <li>Units should be reported when relevant</li>

        <li>Totals often improve interpretability</li>

        <li>Frequencies and percentages are often reported together</li>

        <li>Missing values should be handled transparently</li>

        <li>Good tables should be understandable on their own</li>

      </ul>
