    <h2>Why Research Questions Matter</h2>

    <p>
      Statistics is not primarily about formulas or software.
    </p>

    <p>
      It is about answering questions using data.
    </p>

    <p>
      The research question determines:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>What data should be collected</li>

      <li>Which variables are important</li>

      <li>Which statistical methods are appropriate</li>

      <li>How results should be interpreted</li>

    </ul>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Main idea:</strong>

      <p>
        A clear research question guides every major decision in a statistical analysis.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>What Is a Research Question?</h2>

    <p>
      A research question is a focused question that can be investigated using data.
    </p>

    <p>
      It identifies the phenomenon of interest and clarifies what the analyst hopes to learn.
    </p>

    <p>
      Good research questions are specific enough to guide analysis while remaining meaningful and relevant.
    </p>

    <h2>Examples of Research Questions</h2>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>Research Area</th>
            <th>Example Question</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>Education</td>
            <td>Do students who study more achieve higher exam scores?</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Medicine</td>
            <td>Does a new treatment improve recovery rates?</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Business</td>
            <td>Does advertising increase sales?</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Public Health</td>
            <td>Is smoking associated with lung disease?</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
      Each question suggests different variables, data structures, and statistical methods.
    </p>

    <h2>From Curiosity to Analysis</h2>

    <p>
      Research often begins with a broad topic.
    </p>

    <p>
      Analysts then refine that topic into a question that can be investigated systematically.
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Broad Topic: Student Success
      </p>

      <p>
        Research Question: Is study time associated with exam performance?
      </p>

    </div>

    <p>
      The refined question provides a clearer analytical direction.
    </p>

    <h2>Good Questions Are Specific</h2>

    <p>
      Vague questions can create confusion and make analysis difficult.
    </p>

    <p>
      Consider the difference:
    </p>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>Question</th>
            <th>Quality</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>How do students learn?</td>
            <td>Very broad</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Is study time associated with exam scores?</td>
            <td>Focused and measurable</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
      Statistical methods work best when questions are clearly defined.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Key insight:</strong>

      <p>
        Better questions often lead to better analyses.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Research Questions and Variables</h2>

    <p>
      Every research question involves variables.
    </p>

    <p>
      For example:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Does exercise affect blood pressure?
      </p>
    </div>

    <p>
      This question involves:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Exercise (predictor)</li>

      <li>Blood pressure (outcome)</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Identifying variables is one of the first steps in method selection.
    </p>

    <h2>Different Types of Questions</h2>

    <p>
      Research questions can focus on different goals.
    </p>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>Question Type</th>
            <th>Example</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>Description</td>
            <td>What is the average income?</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Comparison</td>
            <td>Do two groups differ?</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Association</td>
            <td>Are two variables related?</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Prediction</td>
            <td>Can future outcomes be predicted?</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
      Different question types often require different statistical methods.
    </p>

    <h2>Questions Drive Method Selection</h2>

    <p>
      Consider these two questions:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>What is the average age of customers?</li>

      <li>Are customer ages different across regions?</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Although both involve age, they require different analyses because the questions differ.
    </p>

    <p>
      The research question determines the statistical approach.
    </p>

    <h2>Questions Before Methods</h2>

    <p>
      Beginning analysts sometimes choose a statistical method before clarifying the question.
    </p>

    <p>
      This can lead to inappropriate analyses.
    </p>

    <p>
      A more effective workflow is:
    </p>

    <ol>

      <li>Define the question.</li>

      <li>Identify the variables.</li>

      <li>Examine the data structure.</li>

      <li>Select an appropriate method.</li>

    </ol>

    <p>
      The question should come first.
    </p>

    <h2>Questions and Causality</h2>

    <p>
      Some questions focus on association.
    </p>

    <p>
      Others focus on causation.
    </p>

    <p>
      For example:
    </p>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>Question</th>
            <th>Focus</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>Are smoking and lung disease related?</td>
            <td>Association</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Does smoking cause lung disease?</td>
            <td>Causation</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <p>
      The distinction affects study design and interpretation.
    </p>

    <h2>Questions Evolve</h2>

    <p>
      Research questions are not always fixed from the beginning.
    </p>

    <p>
      As analysts learn more about the problem and the available data, questions may be refined.
    </p>

    <p>
      Refinement often improves analytical clarity.
    </p>

    <h2>Common Mistakes</h2>

    <p>
      Poorly formulated questions often suffer from:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Excessive vagueness</li>

      <li>Unclear variables</li>

      <li>Unmeasurable concepts</li>

      <li>Multiple questions combined into one</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Clear questions help avoid these problems.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Important perspective:</strong>

      <p>
        Statistical methods answer questions. They do not create questions.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>The Bigger Picture</h2>

    <p>
      Method selection begins long before any calculations are performed.
    </p>

    <p>
      The research question establishes the purpose of the analysis and guides every subsequent decision.
    </p>

    <p>
      Analysts who begin with clear questions are far more likely to choose appropriate methods and obtain meaningful results.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Core message:</strong>

      <p>
        Research questions provide the foundation for statistical analysis. A clear question guides data collection, variable selection, method choice, and interpretation.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Looking Ahead</h2>

    <p>
      Once a research question has been defined, the next step is identifying and classifying the variables involved.
    </p>

    <p>
      Variable type strongly influences which statistical methods can be used and how data should be analyzed.
    </p>

    <p>
      The next lesson introduces variable types and explains why they play a central role in method selection.
    </p>

      <ul class="bullets">

        <li>Every statistical analysis begins with a research question</li>

        <li>Research questions guide data collection and method selection</li>

        <li>Good questions are focused, measurable, and meaningful</li>

        <li>Different question types require different statistical approaches</li>

        <li>Variables are identified from the research question</li>

        <li>Questions should be clarified before choosing methods</li>
