    <h2>Why Study Designs Differ</h2>

    <p>
      Research questions vary widely.
    </p>

    <p>
      Some studies investigate current conditions, others examine change over time, and some evaluate interventions.
    </p>

    <p>
      Different objectives require different study structures.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Main idea:</strong>

      <p>
        Study designs are frameworks for collecting data in ways that align with specific research goals.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Two Broad Categories</h2>

    <p>
      Most study designs fall into two general groups:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Observational designs</li>

      <li>Experimental designs</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Within these categories, several common designs appear repeatedly across scientific research.
    </p>

    <h2>Cross-Sectional Studies</h2>

    <p>
      A cross-sectional study collects data at a single point in time.
    </p>

    <p>
      Researchers obtain a snapshot of a population and analyze characteristics as they currently exist.
    </p>

    <p>
      Examples include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Public opinion surveys</li>

      <li>Health surveys</li>

      <li>Market research studies</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Cross-sectional designs are among the most common observational approaches.
    </p>

    <h2>Advantages of Cross-Sectional Studies</h2>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Relatively inexpensive</li>

      <li>Quick to conduct</li>

      <li>Useful for describing populations</li>

      <li>Suitable for large samples</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      However, they provide limited information about changes over time.
    </p>

    <h2>Longitudinal Studies</h2>

    <p>
      Longitudinal studies collect data from the same individuals repeatedly over time.
    </p>

    <p>
      Researchers can observe how outcomes evolve and how variables change.
    </p>

    <p>
      Examples include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Child development studies</li>

      <li>Aging research</li>

      <li>Long-term health monitoring</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      These studies provide insight into temporal patterns and trends.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Key insight:</strong>

      <p>
        Cross-sectional studies provide snapshots, while longitudinal studies provide movies.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Cohort Studies</h2>

    <p>
      A cohort study follows a group of individuals who share a common characteristic.
    </p>

    <p>
      Researchers track outcomes over time and compare different exposures or experiences.
    </p>

    <p>
      Example:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Following smokers and non-smokers for several years to compare disease rates.
      </p>
    </div>

    <p>
      Cohort studies are common in epidemiology and public health.
    </p>

    <h2>Prospective and Retrospective Cohorts</h2>

    <p>
      Cohort studies may be:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Prospective — following participants into the future</li>

      <li>Retrospective — using existing records from the past</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      Both approaches attempt to connect exposures with later outcomes.
    </p>

    <h2>Case-Control Studies</h2>

    <p>
      Case-control studies begin with an outcome and work backward.
    </p>

    <p>
      Researchers identify:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Cases (individuals with the outcome)</li>

      <li>Controls (individuals without the outcome)</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      They then compare past exposures between groups.
    </p>

    <p>
      Example:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Comparing smoking histories among people with and without lung cancer.
      </p>
    </div>

    <h2>Advantages of Case-Control Studies</h2>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Efficient for rare outcomes</li>

      <li>Often less expensive than cohort studies</li>

      <li>Require fewer participants</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      However, they can be vulnerable to recall and selection biases.
    </p>

    <h2>Randomized Controlled Trials</h2>

    <p>
      Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) are among the most influential experimental designs.
    </p>

    <p>
      Participants are randomly assigned to different conditions.
    </p>

    <p>
      Example:
    </p>

    <div class="example-box">

      <p>
        Randomly assigning patients to receive a new treatment or a standard treatment.
      </p>
    </div>

    <p>
      Randomization helps balance confounding variables between groups.
    </p>

    <h2>Why RCTs Are Powerful</h2>

    <p>
      Randomized trials provide strong evidence regarding causal relationships.
    </p>

    <p>
      Because researchers control treatment assignment, alternative explanations become less likely.
    </p>

    <p>
      For this reason, RCTs are often considered the gold standard for evaluating interventions.
    </p>

    <h2>Quasi-Experimental Designs</h2>

    <p>
      Sometimes random assignment is impossible.
    </p>

    <p>
      Researchers may still evaluate interventions using quasi-experimental designs.
    </p>

    <p>
      These studies resemble experiments but lack full randomization.
    </p>

    <p>
      Although useful, they generally provide weaker causal evidence than randomized trials.
    </p>

    <h2>A Comparison of Common Designs</h2>

    <div class="table-wrap">

      <table>

        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>Design</th>
            <th>Primary Purpose</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>

        <tbody>

          <tr>
            <td>Cross-Sectional</td>
            <td>Describe current conditions</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Longitudinal</td>
            <td>Study change over time</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Cohort</td>
            <td>Track exposures and outcomes</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>Case-Control</td>
            <td>Study rare outcomes</td>
          </tr>

          <tr>
            <td>RCT</td>
            <td>Evaluate causal effects</td>
          </tr>

        </tbody>

      </table>

    </div>

    <h2>Choosing a Design</h2>

    <p>
      The choice of design depends on:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Research objectives</li>

      <li>Ethical constraints</li>

      <li>Available resources</li>

      <li>Time requirements</li>

      <li>Data availability</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      No single design is optimal for every situation.
    </p>

    <h2>Design Influences Evidence Quality</h2>

    <p>
      Different study designs provide different levels of evidence.
    </p>

    <p>
      In general:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">

      <li>Randomized experiments support stronger causal conclusions</li>

      <li>Observational studies often provide stronger real-world context</li>

      <li>Longitudinal studies provide stronger temporal evidence than snapshots</li>

    </ul>

    <p>
      The best design depends on the research question.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Important perspective:</strong>

      <p>
        Strong study design improves the credibility of conclusions long before statistical analysis begins.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>The Bigger Picture</h2>

    <p>
      Study designs determine how evidence is generated.
    </p>

    <p>
      Whether researchers collect a snapshot, follow participants over time, compare cases and controls, or conduct randomized experiments, the design shapes what can be learned from the data.
    </p>

    <p>
      Understanding common study designs helps analysts critically evaluate research findings and choose appropriate approaches for future investigations.
    </p>

    <div class="concept-box">

      <strong>Core message:</strong>

      <p>
        Cross-sectional, longitudinal, cohort, case-control, and experimental designs each serve different research purposes. Selecting an appropriate design is one of the most important decisions in applied statistics.
      </p>

    </div>

    <h2>Looking Ahead</h2>

    <p>
      Even a well-designed study can produce misleading conclusions if the sample does not adequately represent the population of interest.
    </p>

    <p>
      Sampling methods play a critical role in determining whether results can be generalized.
    </p>

    <p>
      The next lesson explores sampling and representativeness and explains why who is included in a study matters so much.
    </p>
