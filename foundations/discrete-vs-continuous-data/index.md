<section class="section">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>Why this topic matters</h2>

    <p>
      Many statistical methods assume that data are either discrete or continuous.
    </p>

    <p>
      Confusing these two types can lead to inappropriate summaries,
      incorrect visualizations, and invalid models.
    </p>

    <p>
      Understanding this distinction early helps prevent errors
      that appear later in statistical analysis.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="section">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>What discrete means</h2>

    <p>
      Discrete data take on distinct, separate values.
      There are gaps between possible values.
    </p>

    <p>
      Discrete values often arise from counting,
      such as the number of customers or events.
    </p>

    <p>
      You cannot meaningfully observe values
      between two consecutive discrete outcomes.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="section">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>What continuous means</h2>

    <p>
      Continuous data can take on any value
      within a given range.
    </p>

    <p>
      They arise from measurement rather than counting,
      such as time, height, or temperature.
    </p>

    <p>
      Between any two values, additional values
      are conceptually possible.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="section">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>Measurement vs conceptual continuity</h2>

    <p>
      Continuous quantities are often recorded
      using discrete measurement instruments.
    </p>

    <p>
      Rounding or limited precision does not make
      the underlying variable discrete.
    </p>

    <p>
      The distinction depends on the concept being measured,
      not on how many decimal places are recorded.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="section">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>Why this matters for later methods</h2>

    <p>
      Different statistical methods assume different
      types of data.
    </p>

    <p>
      Some models are designed for counts,
      while others assume continuous variation.
    </p>

    <p>
      Choosing methods without considering this distinction
      can invalidate results and conclusions.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="section section-soft">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>Key idea to remember</h2>

    <p>
      Discrete and continuous data differ conceptually,
      and this difference determines which analyses are appropriate.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="section section-next">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>Simple summary</h2>

    <p>
      Discrete data arise from counting,
      while continuous data arise from measurement.
      Understanding the difference is essential
      for correct statistical analysis.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>
