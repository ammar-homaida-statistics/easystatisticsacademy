<section class="section">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>Why this topic matters</h2>

    <p>
      Data are not abstract objects.
      They are collected from people, systems, and real environments.
    </p>

    <p>
      Decisions about how data are collected
      can affect individuals, groups, and entire populations.
    </p>

    <p>
      Ethical failures can produce harm
      even when statistical methods are technically correct.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>



<section class="section">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>Informed consent</h2>

    <p>
      Informed consent means that participants understand:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">
      <li>What data are being collected</li>
      <li>Why they are being collected</li>
      <li>How the data will be used</li>
    </ul>

    <p>
      Consent must be voluntary and based on clear information,
      not hidden terms or vague explanations.
    </p>

    <p>
      When participants lack real choice or understanding,
      consent becomes questionable.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>



<section class="section">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>Privacy and anonymity</h2>

    <p>
      <strong>Privacy</strong> concerns how personal information is protected.
    </p>

    <p>
      <strong>Anonymity</strong> means that individuals cannot be identified
      from the data.
    </p>

    <p>
      These are not the same.
      Data can be anonymized and still pose privacy risks.
    </p>

    <p>
      Combining datasets or using additional information
      can sometimes reveal identities indirectly.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>



<section class="section">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>Data misuse</h2>

    <p>
      Data misuse occurs when data are used in ways
      that participants did not expect or agree to.
    </p>

    <p>
      Examples include:
    </p>

    <ul class="bullets">
      <li>Using data for purposes unrelated to the original study</li>
      <li>Sharing data without permission</li>
      <li>Producing misleading or exaggerated conclusions</li>
      <li>Building systems that disadvantage certain groups</li>
    </ul>

    <p>
      Ethical responsibility continues after data are collected.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>



<section class="section">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>The illusion of “safe data”</h2>

    <p>
      It is often assumed that removing names or identifiers
      makes data safe.
    </p>

    <p>
      In reality, data can often be re-identified
      when combined with other sources.
    </p>

    <p>
      Ethical data handling requires thinking beyond
      simple technical fixes.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>



<section class="section">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>Why ethics is a statistical issue</h2>

    <p>
      Statistical decisions affect what data are collected,
      how variables are defined, and how results are reported.
    </p>

    <p>
      Ethics is not separate from statistics.
      It shapes study design, interpretation, and conclusions.
    </p>

    <p>
      Responsible statistical practice requires both
      technical accuracy and ethical judgment.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>



<section class="section section-soft">
  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>Key idea to remember</h2>

    <p>
      Ethical data collection is essential.
      Correct analysis does not justify unethical data practices.
    </p>
  </div>
</section>

  <div class="content-narrow">
    <h2>Simple summary</h2>

    <p>
      Ethics determines how data are collected and used.
      Consent, privacy, and responsible interpretation
      are essential for trustworthy statistics.
    </p>
