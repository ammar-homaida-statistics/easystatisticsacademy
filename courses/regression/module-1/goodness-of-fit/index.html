Lesson 1.6 — Goodness of Fit

R² is the most quoted number in applied regression and one of the most misread. This lesson derives it, interprets it, and shows with four famous data sets why a number cannot replace a plot.

1 Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. prove the decomposition \(\mathrm{SST} = \mathrm{SSR} + \mathrm{SSE}\) and interpret it geometrically;
  2. define \(R^2\), prove that \(R^2 = r^2 = [\operatorname{corr}(y, \hat y)]^2\) in simple regression, and interpret it correctly;
  3. use the residual standard error \(\hat\sigma\) as a measure of fit in the units of \(y\);
  4. explain what \(R^2\) does not measure, using Anscombe’s quartet and the effect of the range of \(x\);
  5. explain why \(R^2\) values for different response scales cannot be compared directly.

2 Motivation

The Auto MPG fit of mpg on weight has \(R^2 = 0.692\). Is that good? Is the straight line the right model? Would a different sample of cars give a similar value? \(R^2\) is the most quoted number in applied regression and one of the most misread. This lesson derives it, gives it a precise meaning, and shows — with four famous data sets that share the same \(R^2\) — why a number cannot replace a plot.

3 Notation for this lesson

Symbol Meaning
\(\mathrm{SST} = \sum (y_i - \bar y)^2 = S_{yy}\) total sum of squares
\(\mathrm{SSR} = \sum (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2\) regression sum of squares
\(\mathrm{SSE} = \sum (y_i - \hat y_i)^2\) error (residual) sum of squares
\(R^2\) coefficient of determination
\(\hat\sigma = \sqrt{\mathrm{SSE}/(n-2)}\) residual standard error
\(s_y = \sqrt{\mathrm{SST}/(n-1)}\) sample standard deviation of \(y\)

All notation follows the course notation standard (D2: SST = SSR + SSE).

4 Theory

4.1 The analysis-of-variance decomposition

For each observation, \[ \underbrace{y_i - \bar y}_{\text{total deviation}} = \underbrace{\hat y_i - \bar y}_{\text{explained by the line}} + \underbrace{y_i - \hat y_i}_{\text{residual}} . \]

Theorem 1.6.1 (Decomposition of the total sum of squares)

For the least-squares fit with an intercept, \[ \mathrm{SST} = \mathrm{SSR} + \mathrm{SSE}, \qquad \text{with} \quad \mathrm{SSR} = \hat\beta_1^2 S_{xx} = \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}} . \]

Derivation

Square and sum the identity above: \(\mathrm{SST} = \mathrm{SSR} + \mathrm{SSE} + 2\sum (\hat y_i - \bar y)e_i\). The cross term is \(2\sum \hat y_i e_i - 2\bar y \sum e_i = 0\) by Theorem 1.3.2 (a) and (c). Since \(\hat y_i - \bar y = \hat\beta_1(x_i - \bar x)\), \(\mathrm{SSR} = \hat\beta_1^2 S_{xx} = S_{xy}^2/S_{xx}\).

Geometry. In \(\mathbb R^n\), the vector \(\mathbf y - \bar y\mathbf 1\) is the sum of \(\hat{\mathbf y} - \bar y\mathbf 1\) and \(\mathbf e\), and these two vectors are orthogonal (Lesson 1.3). The decomposition is Pythagoras’ theorem.

The identity requires an intercept: without one, \(\sum e_i \neq 0\) in general and the cross term need not vanish (Lesson 1.3, Exercise B2).

4.2 The coefficient of determination

Definition 1.6.1 (\(R^2\))

\[ R^2 = \frac{\mathrm{SSR}}{\mathrm{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{\mathrm{SST}}, \qquad 0 \le R^2 \le 1 . \] \(R^2\) is the proportion of the total variation of \(y\) about its mean that is accounted for by the least-squares line.

Theorem 1.6.2 (\(R^2\) and correlation)

In simple linear regression, \[ R^2 = r^2 = \bigl[\operatorname{corr}(y, \hat y)\bigr]^2 . \]

Derivation

\(R^2 = \mathrm{SSR}/\mathrm{SST} = S_{xy}^2/(S_{xx}S_{yy}) = r^2\) (equivalently \(1 - \mathrm{SSE}/S_{yy} = r^2\) by Theorem 1.2.1). Because \(\hat y_i\) is a linear function of \(x_i\) with slope \(\hat\beta_1\), \(\operatorname{corr}(y, \hat y) = \operatorname{sign}(\hat\beta_1)\,r\) (Theorem 1.2.2), and squaring removes the sign.

The second form, \([\operatorname{corr}(y, \hat y)]^2\), is the one that generalises to multiple regression (Module 2): \(R^2\) measures how closely the fitted values track the observed responses.

4.3 The residual standard error

\(R^2\) is unit-free; \(\hat\sigma\) is in the units of \(y\). They are linked by \[ \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{n-1}{n-2}\, s_y^2\,(1 - R^2), \tag{1.6.1} \] so \(\hat\sigma\) compares the typical scatter around the line with the scatter \(s_y\) around the mean. For prediction, \(\hat\sigma\) is often the more useful number: it says roughly how far observations fall from the line in the units that matter (Lesson 1.8).

4.4 What \(R^2\) does not tell us

Limitations of \(R^2\)

  1. It does not show that the model is correct. A curved relationship can have a high \(R^2\) for a straight line, and data from a correct straight-line model can have a low \(R^2\) if \(\sigma\) is large.
  2. It depends on the range of \(x\). Under the linear model with fixed \(\sigma\), a wider spread of \(x\) gives a larger \(R^2\) for the same slope (Lesson 1.2, Exercise B3).
  3. It is positive by chance. If \(\beta_1 = 0\) and the errors are normal, \(R^2\) has a \(\text{Beta}\bigl(\tfrac12, \tfrac{n-2}{2}\bigr)\) distribution with mean \(1/(n-1)\): about 0.11 for \(n = 10\) (Exercise C2).
  4. It is not comparable across responses. \(R^2\) for mpg and \(R^2\) for \(\log(\text{mpg})\) measure proportions of different variances.
  5. It does not measure predictive accuracy for new data. That is a question for Module 4 (cross-validation).
  6. It never decreases when predictors are added — the motivation for adjusted \(R^2\) in Module 4.

5 Worked examples

5.1 The Auto MPG fit

For mpg on weight (1000 lb), \(n = 398\):

Source Sum of squares
Regression (SSR) 16777.76
Error (SSE) 7474.81
Total (SST) 24252.58

So \(R^2 = 16777.76/24252.58 = 0.6918\), which equals \(r^2 = (-0.8317)^2\). The residual standard error is \(\hat\sigma = 4.34\) mpg, compared with \(s_y = 7.82\) mpg: the line reduces the typical deviation from about 7.8 mpg (around the mean) to about 4.3 mpg (around the line).

R code 
cols <- c("mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
          "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name")
auto <- read.table("data/auto-mpg.data", col.names = cols, na.strings = "?")
x <- auto$weight / 1000; y <- auto$mpg
fit <- lm(y ~ x)

SST <- sum((y - mean(y))^2)
SSE <- sum(resid(fit)^2)
SSR <- sum((fitted(fit) - mean(y))^2)
c(SST = SST, SSR = SSR, SSE = SSE, SSR_plus_SSE = SSR + SSE,
  R2 = SSR / SST, r_squared = cor(x, y)^2, corr_y_yhat_sq = cor(y, fitted(fit))^2,
  sigma_hat = sigma(fit), s_y = sd(y))
anova(fit)                       # the same decomposition as an ANOVA table
Python code 
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

cols = ["mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
        "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name"]
auto = pd.read_csv("data/auto-mpg.data", sep=r"\s+", header=None,
                   names=cols, na_values="?", quotechar='"')
x = auto["weight"].to_numpy() / 1000
y = auto["mpg"].to_numpy()
n = len(y)

def ols(x, y):
    Sxx = np.sum((x - x.mean())**2)
    b1 = np.sum((x - x.mean()) * (y - y.mean())) / Sxx
    return y.mean() - b1 * x.mean(), b1

b0, b1 = ols(x, y)
yhat = b0 + b1 * x
SST = np.sum((y - y.mean())**2)
SSE = np.sum((y - yhat)**2)
SSR = np.sum((yhat - y.mean())**2)
print(f"SST = {SST:.4f}, SSR = {SSR:.4f}, SSE = {SSE:.4f}, SSR + SSE = {SSR + SSE:.4f}")
print(f"R2 = {SSR / SST:.6f}, r^2 = {np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]**2:.6f}, "
      f"corr(y, yhat)^2 = {np.corrcoef(y, yhat)[0, 1]**2:.6f}")
print(f"sigma_hat = {np.sqrt(SSE / (n - 2)):.4f}, s_y = {y.std(ddof=1):.4f}")

Output 

SST = 24252.5755, SSR = 16777.7615, SSE = 7474.8140, SSR + SSE = 24252.5755
R2 = 0.691793, r^2 = 0.691793, corr(y, yhat)^2 = 0.691793
sigma_hat = 4.3446, s_y = 7.8160

The ANOVA table printed by R is the starting point of the \(F\)-test in Lesson 1.7.

5.2 Anscombe’s quartet

Anscombe (1973) constructed four data sets of eleven points each with (to two or three decimals) the same means, variances, correlation, regression line and \(R^2\) — and completely different structure.

Data set \(\hat\beta_0\) \(\hat\beta_1\) \(R^2\) \(\hat\sigma\)
I 3.000 0.500 0.667 1.24
II 3.001 0.500 0.666 1.24
III 3.002 0.500 0.666 1.24
IV 3.002 0.500 0.667 1.24
R code 
# 'anscombe' is a data set built into R
op <- par(mfrow = c(2, 2), mar = c(4, 4, 1, 1))
for (k in 1:4) {
  xk <- anscombe[[paste0("x", k)]]; yk <- anscombe[[paste0("y", k)]]
  fk <- lm(yk ~ xk)
  plot(xk, yk, pch = 19, xlim = c(3, 20), ylim = c(2, 14),
       xlab = paste0("x", k), ylab = paste0("y", k))
  abline(fk, lwd = 2)
  print(round(c(set = k, b0 = coef(fk)[[1]], b1 = coef(fk)[[2]],
                R2 = summary(fk)$r.squared, sigma = sigma(fk)), 4))
}
par(op)
Python code 
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

x4   = np.array([8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 19, 8, 8, 8], dtype=float)

    1: [8.04, 6.95, 7.58, 8.81, 8.33, 9.96, 7.24, 4.26, 10.84, 4.82, 5.68],
    2: [9.14, 8.14, 8.74, 8.77, 9.26, 8.10, 6.13, 3.10, 9.13, 7.26, 4.74],
    3: [7.46, 6.77, 12.74, 7.11, 7.81, 8.84, 6.08, 5.39, 8.15, 6.42, 5.73],
    4: [6.58, 5.76, 7.71, 8.84, 8.47, 7.04, 5.25, 12.50, 5.56, 7.91, 6.89],
}
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(7, 5.5), sharex=True, sharey=True)
grid = np.array([3, 20])
for k, ax in zip(ys, axes.ravel()):
    xk = x4 if k == 4 else x123
    yk = np.array(ys[k])
    a0, a1 = ols(xk, yk)
    ek = yk - a0 - a1 * xk
    R2k = 1 - ek @ ek / np.sum((yk - yk.mean())**2)
    print(f"set {k}: b0 = {a0:.4f}, b1 = {a1:.4f}, R2 = {R2k:.4f}, sigma = {np.sqrt(ek @ ek / 9):.4f}")
    ax.scatter(xk, yk, color="black", s=20)
    ax.plot(grid, a0 + a1 * grid, linewidth=2)
    ax.set_title(f"Data set {k}")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

  • I — a reasonable straight-line relationship with scatter.
  • II — an exact curve; a straight line is the wrong model despite \(R^2 = 0.67\).
  • III — a perfect straight line except for one outlier, which pulls the fitted line.
  • IV — no information about the relationship except from a single high-leverage point; remove it and the slope is not estimable.

The same summary statistics describe four different situations. Always plot the data and the residuals (Module 3).

5.3 The range of \(x\) matters

Restricting the Auto MPG data to cars weighing between 2000 and 3500 lb (246 cars) halves the standard deviation of weight (0.847 → 0.434 thousand lb) and lowers \(R^2\) from 0.692 to 0.442, although \(\hat\sigma\) changes little (4.34 → 4.76 mpg). The slope also changes, from \(-7.68\) to \(-9.74\); under an exactly linear model it would not change systematically — here it does because the true mean function is curved and is steepest in the middle of the weight range (Lesson 1.1). This is Exercise C3.

5.4 Different responses, different \(R^2\)

The fits of Lesson 1.5 on weight gave \(R^2 = 0.692\) for mpg, \(0.767\) for \(\log(\text{mpg})\) and \(0.784\) for litres per 100 km. These numbers do not show which model is best: each is the proportion of a different total sum of squares. Comparing models for different response scales requires other tools (residual plots, back-transformed prediction errors; Modules 3–4).

6 Common misconceptions

Misconceptions

  1. “\(R^2 = 0.69\) means the model is 69% correct.” — \(R^2\) is a proportion of variance accounted for by the fitted line, not a measure of correctness.
  2. “A high \(R^2\) means a straight line is appropriate.” — Anscombe II has \(R^2 = 0.67\) and is an exact curve.
  3. “A low \(R^2\) means \(x\) has no effect on \(y\).” — A precisely estimated, important slope can coexist with a low \(R^2\) when \(\sigma\) is large or the range of \(x\) is narrow.
  4. “The model with the higher \(R^2\) is better.” — Not across different response scales, and not across models with different numbers of predictors (Module 4).
  5. “\(R^2\) is a property of the relationship.” — It is a property of the relationship and the design: the spread of \(x\) in the sample.

7 Key takeaways

  • \(\mathrm{SST} = \mathrm{SSR} + \mathrm{SSE}\) is Pythagoras’ theorem for the orthogonal vectors \(\hat{\mathbf y} - \bar y\mathbf 1\) and \(\mathbf e\); it needs an intercept.
  • \(R^2 = \mathrm{SSR}/\mathrm{SST} = r^2 = [\operatorname{corr}(y, \hat y)]^2\).
  • \(\hat\sigma\) reports the typical size of residuals in the units of \(y\); it is often more useful than \(R^2\).
  • \(R^2\) depends on the range of \(x\), is positive by chance, and says nothing about whether the model is right.
  • Always plot the data: Anscombe’s four data sets share every summary statistic.

8 Exercises

8.1 A. Conceptual

A1 (★). Two studies estimate the same slope of blood pressure on salt intake, with the same \(\hat\sigma\). Study A recruited people with a wide range of salt intakes and reports \(R^2 = 0.40\); Study B recruited only people with moderate intakes and reports \(R^2 = 0.08\). Do the studies contradict each other? Explain.

Solution

No. With the same slope and error variance, \(R^2 = \hat\beta_1^2 S_{xx}/(\hat\beta_1^2 S_{xx} + \mathrm{SSE})\) grows with the spread of \(x\). Study B’s narrow range of salt intake gives a small \(S_{xx}\) and hence a small \(R^2\); the estimated relationship is the same.

A2 (★). Which of Anscombe’s four data sets would you be comfortable summarising by its regression line, and why not the others?

Solution

Only data set I. II is curved (wrong mean function); III has one outlier that shifts the line away from the other ten points, which lie exactly on a different line; IV has a slope determined entirely by one point, with no information about how \(y\) varies with \(x\) elsewhere.

8.2 B. Mathematical

B1 (★★). Prove Theorem 1.6.1 and show that \(\mathrm{SSR} = \hat\beta_1 S_{xy}\).

Solution

See the derivation after Theorem 1.6.1. Since \(\mathrm{SSR} = \hat\beta_1^2 S_{xx}\) and \(\hat\beta_1 S_{xx} = S_{xy}\), \(\mathrm{SSR} = \hat\beta_1 S_{xy}\). Check with Auto MPG: \(\hat\beta_1 S_{xy} = (-7.676610)(-2185.569063) = 16777.76\).

B2 (★★). Prove (1.6.1). Deduce that adding a predictor that increases \(R^2\) only slightly can increase \(\hat\sigma\) in multiple regression, where the denominator becomes \(n - p - 1\).

Solution

\(\hat\sigma^2 = \mathrm{SSE}/(n-2) = \mathrm{SST}(1 - R^2)/(n-2) = (n-1)s_y^2(1 - R^2)/(n-2)\). In multiple regression \(\hat\sigma^2 = (n-1)s_y^2(1 - R^2)/(n-p-1)\): adding a predictor decreases \(1 - R^2\) but also decreases the denominator by one, so \(\hat\sigma^2\) increases if the relative decrease in \(1 - R^2\) is smaller than \(1/(n-p-1)\). This is the idea behind adjusted \(R^2\) (Module 4).

B3 (★★★). Under \(H_0\!: \beta_1 = 0\) with normal errors, it is known that \(R^2 \sim \text{Beta}\bigl(\tfrac12, \tfrac{n-2}{2}\bigr)\). Find \(E(R^2)\) and explain why a “small” \(R^2\) can still be statistically significant when \(n\) is large.

Solution

For \(\text{Beta}(a, b)\) the mean is \(a/(a+b) = \tfrac12 / \tfrac{n-1}{2} = 1/(n-1)\). Under \(H_0\), \(R^2\) concentrates near \(1/(n-1)\), which tends to 0 as \(n\) grows. So for large \(n\) even \(R^2 = 0.05\) is far above what chance produces (e.g. \(n = 398\): \(E(R^2) = 0.0025\)). Statistical significance and the size of \(R^2\) answer different questions. The link between \(R^2\) and the \(F\)-test is made precise in Lesson 1.7.

8.3 C. Computational

C1 (★). Fit the four Anscombe regressions in R (anscombe data) or Python, confirm the table above, and plot the four residual-versus-fitted plots. Describe what each residual plot reveals.

Solution

The four fits agree to about three decimals: \(\hat\beta_0 \approx 3.00\), \(\hat\beta_1 \approx 0.500\), \(R^2 \approx 0.67\), \(\hat\sigma \approx 1.24\). Residual plots: I — random scatter; II — a clear inverted-U pattern (curvature); III — ten small residuals in a linear trend and one large residual (outlier); IV — ten residuals at a single fitted value and one point with residual exactly 0 (it determines the slope).

C2 (★★). \(R^2\) by chance. Simulate 10,000 data sets with \(x = 1, \dots, 10\) and \(y\) independent \(N(0, 1)\) (seed 2026). Compute the mean of \(R^2\) and the proportion of data sets with \(R^2 > 0.3\). Compare with \(1/(n-1)\) and with the Beta tail probability.

Solution (Python) 
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
n, R = 10, 10_000
x = np.arange(1, n + 1, dtype=float)
Y = rng.normal(0, 1, size=(R, n))
r2 = np.array([np.corrcoef(x, yy)[0, 1]**2 for yy in Y])
print(round(r2.mean(), 4), round((r2 > 0.3).mean(), 4),
      round(1 / (n - 1), 4), round(stats.beta.sf(0.3, 0.5, (n - 2) / 2), 4))

Output: mean 0.1117 (theory 0.1111); \(P(R^2 > 0.3)\) = 0.1004 (Beta tail 0.1012). With ten observations and no relationship, one data set in ten has \(R^2 > 0.3\).

C3 (★★). Refit mpg on weight for cars weighing 2000–3500 lb only (lower limit included, upper excluded). Report \(n\), the standard deviation of weight, \(\hat\beta_1\), \(\hat\sigma\) and \(R^2\), and explain the changes.

Solution

\(n = 246\); SD of weight 0.434 (thousand lb) vs 0.847 for all cars; \(\hat\beta_1 = -9.739\); \(\hat\sigma = 4.755\); \(R^2 = 0.442\) (vs 0.692). \(R^2\) falls mainly because the spread of \(x\) is smaller, even though \(\hat\sigma\) is similar. The slope is steeper because the mean function is curved and steepest in this middle range; with a truly linear mean function, restricting the range would leave the slope’s expectation unchanged.
