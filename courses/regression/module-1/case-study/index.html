1 Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. carry out a complete simple regression analysis, from question to report, using every tool of Module 1;
  2. connect each step to the assumptions it needs and check those assumptions with a first look at the residuals;
  3. write a short, honest report: estimates with intervals, interpretation in context, and limitations;
  4. recognise which problems a straight line on one predictor cannot solve, and which later modules address them.

2 The question

How is a car’s weight related to its fuel economy, and how well can fuel economy be predicted from weight alone?

The question has two parts. The first is descriptive (the size and direction of the association). The second is predictive (how precisely mpg can be predicted for a new car). Neither part is causal: the data are observational (Lesson 1.1), so the analysis will not claim what happens if weight is changed.

3 Step 1 — Know the data

Item Value
Source Quinlan (1993), UCI Machine Learning Repository, CC BY 4.0
Units of observation 398 car models, model years 1970–1982
Origin 249 American, 70 European, 79 Japanese
Response mpg: fuel economy, miles per gallon (9.0 to 46.6; mean 23.51, SD 7.82)
Predictor weight: lb (1613 to 5140; mean 2970, SD 847), used in thousands of lb
Missing values none in mpg or weight (six in horsepower, not used here)

Two features matter for interpretation: the cars are models, not a random sample of cars on the road, and the period covers the 1970s fuel crisis, when fuel economy improved quickly. Both limit how far the results generalise.

4 Step 2 — Explore before modelling

Lesson 1.1 estimated \(E(\text{mpg} \mid \text{weight})\) without assumptions using band means: mpg falls from about 33 in the lightest band to about 12 in the heaviest, slightly curved, with a spread that shrinks for heavy cars. The correlation is \(r = -0.832\) (Lesson 1.2). A straight line is a reasonable first model, with two warnings already on record: possible curvature (A1) and non-constant variance (A2).

5 Step 3 — Fit the model

\[ \widehat{\text{mpg}} = 46.317 - 7.677 \times \text{weight (1000 lb)}, \qquad n = 398 . \]

# Step 1: know the data
summary(auto[, c("mpg", "weight")])

# Steps 3-5: fit, fit quality, inference
fit <- lm(mpg ~ w, data = auto)
summary(fit)
confint(fit)

# Step 6: prediction for a 2800-lb car
predict(fit, data.frame(w = 2.8), interval = "confidence")
predict(fit, data.frame(w = 2.8), interval = "prediction")
Python code 
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

cols = ["mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
        "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name"]
auto = pd.read_csv("data/auto-mpg.data", sep=r"\s+", header=None,
                   names=cols, na_values="?", quotechar='"')
x = auto["weight"].to_numpy() / 1000
y = auto["mpg"].to_numpy()
n = len(y)

# Step 1: know the data
print(auto[["mpg", "weight"]].describe().round(2))
print(auto["origin"].value_counts().sort_index().to_dict())

# Steps 3-5: fit, fit quality, inference
Sxx = np.sum((x - x.mean())**2)
b1 = np.sum((x - x.mean()) * (y - y.mean())) / Sxx
b0 = y.mean() - b1 * x.mean()
fitted = b0 + b1 * x
e = y - fitted
s = np.sqrt(e @ e / (n - 2))
se1 = s / np.sqrt(Sxx)
q = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 2)
R2 = 1 - (e @ e) / np.sum((y - y.mean())**2)
print(f"b0 = {b0:.4f}, b1 = {b1:.4f} (se {se1:.4f}), 95% CI ({b1 - q * se1:.4f}, {b1 + q * se1:.4f})")
print(f"t = {b1 / se1:.2f}, R2 = {R2:.4f}, sigma_hat = {s:.4f}")

# Step 6: prediction for a 2800-lb car
x0 = 2.8
h0 = 1 / n + (x0 - x.mean())**2 / Sxx
y0 = b0 + b1 * x0
print(f"x0 = 2800 lb: fit {y0:.3f}, CI ({y0 - q * s * np.sqrt(h0):.3f}, {y0 + q * s * np.sqrt(h0):.3f}), "
      f"PI ({y0 - q * s * np.sqrt(1 + h0):.3f}, {y0 + q * s * np.sqrt(1 + h0):.3f})")

6 Step 4 — Interpret the coefficients

  • Slope (Lesson 1.5): among these car models, a difference of 1000 lb is associated with a mean fuel economy lower by 7.68 mpg.
  • Intercept: 46.3 mpg at weight 0 is an extrapolation far outside 1613–5140 lb and has no practical meaning. Centred at 3000 lb (Lesson 1.5), the intercept would be 23.29 mpg, the estimated mean for 3000-lb models.

8 Step 6 — Prediction

For a 2800-lb model:

  • estimated mean fuel economy: 24.82 mpg, 95% CI (24.39, 25.26);
  • one new model: 95% PI (16.27, 33.38).

The mean is pinned down to about ±0.4 mpg; a single car can be anywhere in a band about 17 mpg wide (Lesson 1.8).

9 Step 7 — Check the assumptions: a first look at the residuals

Every number in Steps 5–6 depends on A1 (straight-line mean) and A2 (constant variance), and the prediction interval also on A4. The residuals \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\) are our window on the errors. If the model were right, residuals plotted against weight would scatter evenly around zero with constant spread.

R code 
# Uses auto and fit from Section 5, Step 3 (run that code first).
e <- resid(fit)
six <- cut(auto$w, quantile(auto$w, seq(0, 1, length.out = 7)), include.lowest = TRUE)
round(rbind(mean_resid = tapply(e, six, mean), sd_resid = tapply(e, six, sd)), 2)

op <- par(mfrow = c(1, 2), mar = c(4, 4, 1, 1))

  1. Curvature (A1). The mean residual is positive at both ends and negative in the middle: a U-shape. The straight line underestimates mpg for the lightest and heaviest cars and overestimates it in between.
  2. Non-constant variance (A2). The residual SD falls from more than 5 mpg for light cars to about 2 mpg for heavy cars. This is why the prediction intervals cover 90% of light cars and 99% of heavy ones (Lesson 1.8).
  3. Unusual cars. The five largest residuals (+14 to +16.5 mpg) are all 1980–1982 models, and three are diesels (e.g. vw dasher (diesel), 43.4 mpg at 2335 lb). Fuel type and model year affect mpg but are not in the model — an omitted-variable signal.

10 Step 8 — Report

A report should state the model, the estimates with uncertainty, the interpretation, and the limitations, in that order.

Example report

We analysed fuel economy and weight for 398 car models from 1970–1982 (Auto MPG data). Fuel economy decreased with weight: in a simple linear regression, each additional 1000 lb was associated with a mean fuel economy 7.7 mpg lower (95% CI 7.2 to 8.2; \(R^2 = 0.69\), residual SD 4.3 mpg). For a 2800-lb model the estimated mean was 24.8 mpg (95% CI 24.4 to 25.3), while a 95% prediction interval for a single new model was 16.3 to 33.4 mpg.

These results describe an association in observational data and should not be read as the effect of changing a car’s weight. Residual analysis showed that the relationship is curved and that variability decreases with weight; the reported standard errors and prediction intervals are therefore approximate, and the prediction intervals are too narrow for light cars and too wide for heavy ones. The largest deviations were 1980–1982 models, three of them diesels, suggesting that model year and fuel type, which the model ignores, also matter.

12 Common misconceptions

Misconceptions

  1. “A high \(R^2\) and a huge \(t\)-statistic mean the analysis is finished.” — Steps 5–6 rest on Step 7. Here the fit is strong but the model is visibly wrong in shape and variance.
  2. “Residual checks are optional extras.” — They are the only evidence about A1–A4, on which every interval depends.
  3. “Limitations weaken a report.” — They make it credible. Reviewers trust analyses that state what they cannot show.
  4. “Since the line is imperfect, the analysis is useless.” — The direction and approximate size of the association are clear; the limitations tell us what to improve.

13 Key takeaways

  • A complete analysis runs: question → data → exploration → fit → interpretation → inference → prediction → residual check → report.
  • The residual check comes before trusting intervals and tests, not after.
  • For Auto MPG, weight is strongly associated with fuel economy, but the relationship is curved, the variance is not constant, and other variables matter.
  • A good report gives estimates with intervals, interprets them in context, and states its limitations plainly.

14 Exercises

14.1 A. Conceptual

A1 (★). Rewrite this sentence from a student report so that it is correct: “Our model proves that adding 1000 lb to a car reduces its fuel economy by exactly 7.68 mpg (p < 0.001).”

Solution

“Among these car models, cars 1000 lb heavier had, on average, a fuel economy about 7.7 mpg lower (95% CI 7.2 to 8.2 mpg lower).” Removed: “proves”, “adding … reduces” (causal, about one car), “exactly” (ignores uncertainty); the \(p\)-value adds little once the interval is given.

A2 (★★). The five largest residuals all belong to 1980–1982 models, three of them diesels. Explain why this suggests an omitted variable, and why simply deleting these five cars would be a poor response.

Solution

The residuals are not random: they share characteristics (fuel type, model year) that are related to mpg and not in the model, so part of their effect is left in the errors (and, if those characteristics are correlated with weight, it biases the slope; Module 2). Deleting them hides the evidence instead of modelling it, makes the sample less representative, and the results would apply only to the remaining cars. Module 3 discusses when, if ever, removing observations is justified.

14.2 B. Mathematical

B1 (★★). Show that the residuals always average zero overall (Lesson 1.3). Why, then, can the mean residual within a range of weights be far from zero, and what does a systematic pattern of such means indicate?

Solution

\(\sum e_i = 0\) is the first normal equation, so the overall mean is zero by construction, and \(\sum x_i e_i = 0\) forces the residuals to have no linear trend in \(x\). Nothing forces the mean to be zero within subsets of \(x\). If the true mean function is curved, the line fits the average linear trend, leaving positive residuals where the curve lies above the line and negative where below: a systematic (here U-shaped) pattern in the subset means indicates a misspecified mean function (A1 fails).

14.3 C. Computational — project

C1 (★★★). Your own case study. Repeat Steps 1–9 for mpg on horsepower (392 cars with horsepower recorded). Report the estimates with 95% CIs, \(R^2\) and \(\hat\sigma\); produce the residual plot with sixth-means; and write a report of at most 200 words with limitations.

Solution (key results)

\(n = 392\); \(\hat\beta_0 = 39.936\), \(\hat\beta_1 = -0.1578\) mpg per hp (se 0.00645; 95% CI −0.1705 to −0.1452); \(R^2 = 0.606\); \(\hat\sigma = 4.906\) mpg. Mean residuals by horsepower sixth: +3.69, +0.47, −1.29, −3.01, −1.14, +2.51 — a stronger U-shape than for weight, so curvature is even more pronounced. A good report states that each additional 10 hp is associated with about 1.6 mpg lower mean fuel economy, that the relationship is clearly curved (a straight line misfits at both ends), that six cars were excluded for missing horsepower, and that the data are observational.

C2 (★★). Fit mpg on displacement (cubic inches). Compare \(R^2\) and \(\hat\sigma\) with the weight and horsepower models. Can you conclude which predictor is “the most important cause” of fuel economy? Why or why not?

Solution

Displacement: \(\hat\beta_1 = -0.0603\) mpg per cubic inch, \(R^2 = 0.647\), \(\hat\sigma = 4.65\) (\(n = 398\)). Comparison: weight \(R^2 = 0.692\), \(\hat\sigma = 4.34\) (\(n = 398\)); horsepower \(R^2 = 0.606\), \(\hat\sigma = 4.91\) (\(n = 392\), a slightly different sample). Weight gives the best single-predictor straight-line fit, but this does not establish which variable is the most important cause: the predictors are strongly correlated with each other (e.g. \(r = 0.933\) between weight and displacement), the data are observational, and each simple regression mixes the predictor’s association with that of the others. Separating them requires multiple regression (Module 2) and, given the strong correlations, the methods of Module 5.
