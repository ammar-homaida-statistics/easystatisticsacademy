1 Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. distinguish estimating the mean response \(E(y \mid x_0)\) from predicting a new observation \(y_0\);
  2. derive the variances of \(\hat y_0\) and of the prediction error \(y_0 - \hat y_0\), and the resulting confidence and prediction intervals;
  3. explain why both intervals widen away from \(\bar x\), and why a prediction interval never shrinks to zero width;
  4. distinguish pointwise intervals from simultaneous bands (Working–Hotelling);
  5. explain why prediction intervals depend on normality much more than confidence intervals, and check intervals against data.

2 Motivation

Two questions about a 3000-lb car sound alike but are very different:

  • What is the average fuel economy of all 3000-lb cars? — a question about \(E(y \mid x_0)\).
  • What will be the fuel economy of the next 3000-lb car we test? — a question about one new value \(y_0\).

The first can be answered more and more precisely as data accumulate. The second cannot: even if we knew the regression line exactly, individual cars would still scatter around it. This lesson builds the two intervals, shows exactly where the difference comes from, and checks how well they work on real data.

3 Notation for this lesson

Symbol Meaning
\(x_0\) a given value of the predictor
\(\mu_0 = E(y \mid x_0) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_0\) mean response at \(x_0\)
\(\hat y_0 = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_0\) estimate of \(\mu_0\), and point prediction of \(y_0\)
\(y_0 = \mu_0 + \varepsilon_0\) a new observation at \(x_0\), with \(\varepsilon_0\) independent of the sample
\(h_0 = \dfrac{1}{n} + \dfrac{(x_0 - \bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\) the factor that controls the precision of \(\hat y_0\)

All notation follows the course notation standard.

4 Theory

Throughout, assumptions A1–A4 hold, and the new error \(\varepsilon_0 \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\) is independent of \(\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n\).

4.1 Estimating the mean response

Theorem 1.8.1 (Confidence interval for the mean response)

\(\hat y_0\) is unbiased for \(\mu_0\) with \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat y_0) = \sigma^2 h_0\), and \[ \frac{\hat y_0 - \mu_0}{\hat\sigma\sqrt{h_0}} \sim t_{n-2}. \] A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu_0\) is \[ \hat y_0 \ \pm\ t_{n-2;\,1-\alpha/2}\ \hat\sigma\sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0 - \bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}\, . \]

Derivation

\(\hat y_0 = \bar y + \hat\beta_1(x_0 - \bar x)\), so \(E(\hat y_0) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\bar x + \beta_1(x_0 - \bar x) = \mu_0\). Because \(\operatorname{Cov}(\bar y, \hat\beta_1) = 0\) (Lesson 1.4), \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat y_0) = \sigma^2/n + (x_0 - \bar x)^2\sigma^2/S_{xx} = \sigma^2 h_0\). Under A4, \(\hat y_0\) is normal and is a function of \((\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1)\), hence independent of \(\mathrm{SSE}\) (Theorem 1.7.1). The pivot is therefore a standard normal over an independent \(\sqrt{\chi^2_{n-2}/(n-2)}\): \(t_{n-2}\).

4.2 Predicting a new observation

Theorem 1.8.2 (Prediction interval)

The prediction error \(y_0 - \hat y_0\) has mean 0 and variance \(\sigma^2(1 + h_0)\), and \[ \frac{y_0 - \hat y_0}{\hat\sigma\sqrt{1 + h_0}} \sim t_{n-2}. \] A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) prediction interval for \(y_0\) is \[ \hat y_0 \ \pm\ t_{n-2;\,1-\alpha/2}\ \hat\sigma\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0 - \bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}\, . \]

Derivation

\(y_0 - \hat y_0 = (\mu_0 - \hat y_0) + \varepsilon_0\). The two terms are independent, because \(\hat y_0\) depends only on \(\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n\). Hence \(E(y_0 - \hat y_0) = 0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(y_0 - \hat y_0) = \sigma^2 h_0 + \sigma^2\). Under A4 the error is normal; it is independent of \(\mathrm{SSE}\) (which depends only on the sample residuals and is independent of \(\hat y_0\), and \(\varepsilon_0\) is independent of the whole sample). The pivot is therefore \(t_{n-2}\).

4.3 Comparing the two intervals

The only difference is the “1 +” under the square root. It is the variance of the new error \(\varepsilon_0\), which no amount of data can remove.

  • Width and \(n\). As \(n \to \infty\) with the design spreading out, \(h_0 \to 0\): the confidence interval shrinks to the point \(\mu_0\), but the prediction interval approaches \(\mu_0 \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma\) — about \(\pm 1.96\sigma\) for 95%.
  • Width and \(x_0\). Both intervals are narrowest at \(x_0 = \bar x\) and widen as \(x_0\) moves away from it, because the uncertainty in the slope is multiplied by \((x_0 - \bar x)\). For the confidence interval this effect is proportionally large; for the prediction interval it is usually small within the data range.
  • Extrapolation. Outside the observed \(x\) range the formulas still give a number, but they assume the straight line continues — something the data cannot confirm (Lesson 1.5).

4.4 Pointwise intervals and simultaneous bands

Each interval above has 95% coverage at one chosen \(x_0\). Drawing the intervals at every \(x\) produces a band, but the probability that the true line lies inside the whole band is less than 95%. The Working–Hotelling band \[ \hat y(x) \ \pm\ \sqrt{2F_{2,\,n-2;\,1-\alpha}}\ \hat\sigma\sqrt{h(x)} \qquad \text{for all } x \] contains the entire true regression line with probability \(1-\alpha\). It has the same shape as the pointwise confidence band, but uses a larger multiplier (for \(n = 398\) and 95%: 2.457 instead of 1.966). For a small number \(k\) of pre-specified points, a Bonferroni correction (\(t_{n-2;\,1-\alpha/(2k)}\)) is often narrower.

4.5 The role of normality

The confidence interval for \(\mu_0\) relies on the distribution of \(\hat y_0\), an average of the \(y_i\): by the central limit theorem it is approximately normal in large samples whatever the error distribution. The prediction interval relies on the distribution of the single new error \(\varepsilon_0\), and no averaging helps. If the errors are skewed, a symmetric prediction interval will miss mostly on one side, however large \(n\) is (Exercise C2).

Python code 
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

x = np.array([2.226, 3.84, 3.235, 2.897, 2.842, 4.367, 4.768, 2.221, 3.885, 2.644, 4.984, 4.819, 3.826,
              4.235, 3.403, 4.491, 3.169, 2.786, 2.573, 2.392, 3.44, 3.108, 3.921, 1.645, 3.167])
y = np.array([36.16, 13.58, 26.48, 18.77, 24.81, 7.7, 14.69, 32.24, 20.64, 22.1, 10.36, 6.81, 14.71,
              15.42, 23.3, 12.24, 19.08, 21.25, 31.97, 29.96, 22.39, 30.8, 12.54, 43.57, 34.22])

def slr_intervals(x, y, x0, level=0.95):
    """Fitted values, confidence intervals for the mean and prediction intervals at x0."""
    n = len(y)
    Sxx = np.sum((x - x.mean())**2)
    b1 = np.sum((x - x.mean()) * (y - y.mean())) / Sxx
    b0 = y.mean() - b1 * x.mean()
    s = np.sqrt(np.sum((y - b0 - b1 * x)**2) / (n - 2))
    q = stats.t.ppf(1 - (1 - level) / 2, n - 2)
    x0 = np.atleast_1d(np.asarray(x0, dtype=float))
    h0 = 1 / n + (x0 - x.mean())**2 / Sxx
    fit = b0 + b1 * x0
    return dict(fit=fit,
                ci=(fit - q * s * np.sqrt(h0), fit + q * s * np.sqrt(h0)),
                pi=(fit - q * s * np.sqrt(1 + h0), fit + q * s * np.sqrt(1 + h0)))

r = slr_intervals(x, y, [3.0, 5.0])
for j, x0 in enumerate([3.0, 5.0]):
    print(f"x0 = {x0}: fit = {r['fit'][j]:.4f}, "
          f"95% CI ({r['ci'][0][j]:.4f}, {r['ci'][1][j]:.4f}), "
          f"95% PI ({r['pi'][0][j]:.4f}, {r['pi'][1][j]:.4f}), "
          f"true mean = {46 - 7.7 * x0:.2f}")

  • At \(x_0 = 3.0\) the confidence interval misses the true mean 22.90. This sample’s slope estimate (\(-9.30\)) is unusually steep (Lesson 1.7), and in about 5% of samples a 95% interval misses — this is one of them at this \(x_0\). At \(x_0 = 5.0\) the interval covers the truth.
  • The confidence interval is about four times wider at 5.0 than at 3.0 (8.1 vs 4.3 units), because 5.0 is far from \(\bar x = 3.40\).
  • The prediction intervals are much wider (about 20 units) and change little with \(x_0\): their width is dominated by \(\sigma\).
  • The lower prediction limit at \(x_0 = 5.0\) is negative — impossible for fuel economy. The straight-line normal model does not know that mpg is positive; near the edge of the data the formula can give impossible values.

Keeping the 25 design points fixed and simulating 20,000 new data sets and new observations at \(x_0 = 3.0\) (seed 2026):

With normal errors both intervals achieve their nominal coverage, missing equally often on each side. 6.1 How well do the prediction intervals work here?

Checking each of the 398 cars against its own 95% prediction interval (an in-sample, approximate check):

Cars All Lightest third Middle third Heaviest third
Inside their 95% PI 0.942 0.896 0.939 0.992

Overall coverage looks fine, but it hides a systematic failure: the interval is too narrow for light cars and too wide for heavy cars, because it uses one \(\hat\sigma\) for all weights while the real spread shrinks with weight (Lesson 1.1). For a buyer of a light car, the advertised “95%” interval is closer to 90%. Prediction intervals are only as good as the variance assumption behind them; Module 3 shows how to model non-constant variance or transform the response.

7 Common misconceptions

Misconceptions

  1. “The confidence band shows where 95% of the data should fall.” — No. It describes uncertainty about the mean; most data points lie outside it. The prediction band is the one about individual observations.
  2. “With enough data, prediction intervals become very narrow.” — No. They approach \(\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma\), not zero.
  3. “A 95% band from pointwise intervals contains the whole true line with 95% probability.” — Only simultaneous bands (Working–Hotelling) do that.
  4. “If the overall coverage is 95%, the prediction intervals are fine.” — Coverage can be correct on average and wrong for every subgroup, as the Auto MPG check shows.
  5. “Large \(n\) protects prediction intervals from non-normal errors.” — It protects confidence intervals for the mean, not prediction intervals for single observations.

8 Key takeaways

  • Estimating \(E(y \mid x_0)\) and predicting \(y_0\) use the same point value \(\hat y_0\) but different intervals.
  • \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat y_0) = \sigma^2 h_0\); \(\operatorname{Var}(y_0 - \hat y_0) = \sigma^2(1 + h_0)\), with \(h_0 = 1/n + (x_0 - \bar x)^2/S_{xx}\).
  • Both intervals are narrowest at \(\bar x\); only the confidence interval shrinks to zero as \(n\) grows.
  • Pointwise intervals are not simultaneous bands; use Working–Hotelling (or Bonferroni) for statements about the whole line.
  • Prediction intervals depend directly on normality and constant variance; check them in subgroups, not only overall.

9 Exercises

9.1 A. Conceptual

A1 (★). For each question, say whether a confidence interval for the mean or a prediction interval is needed: (a) the average fuel economy of a manufacturer’s 3000-lb models; (b) the fuel economy of the specific 3000-lb car a customer is about to buy; (c) a regulator’s estimate of the expected fuel economy of 3500-lb cars.

Solution
  1. Confidence interval for the mean (an average over many cars). (b) Prediction interval (one new car). (c) Confidence interval for the mean (expected value).

A2 (★). Explain in one or two sentences why a prediction interval can never be narrower than about \(\pm 1.96\,\sigma\) (for 95%), however much data is collected.

Solution

Even if the line were known exactly, the new observation still deviates from it by its own error \(\varepsilon_0\), which has standard deviation \(\sigma\). Data reduce uncertainty about the line (\(h_0 \to 0\)), not the variability of individual observations.

9.2 B. Mathematical

B1 (★★). Prove that \(\operatorname{Var}(y_0 - \hat y_0) = \sigma^2(1 + h_0)\), stating exactly where independence of \(\varepsilon_0\) from the sample is used.

Solution

\(y_0 - \hat y_0 = \varepsilon_0 + (\mu_0 - \hat y_0)\). \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_0) = \sigma^2\); \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat y_0) = \sigma^2 h_0\) (Theorem 1.8.1). The covariance \(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_0, \hat y_0)\) is zero because \(\hat y_0\) is a linear function of \(y_1, \dots, y_n\) and \(\varepsilon_0\) is uncorrelated with \(\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n\) — the only place independence (uncorrelatedness) of the new error is used. Summing gives \(\sigma^2(1 + h_0)\).

B2 (★★). Show that the ratio of the widths of the prediction and confidence intervals at the same \(x_0\) is \(\sqrt{(1 + h_0)/h_0}\), and evaluate it for Auto MPG at 3000 lb (\(h_0 = 0.002515\)).

Solution

Both widths are \(2t_{n-2;1-\alpha/2}\hat\sigma\) times \(\sqrt{1+h_0}\) and \(\sqrt{h_0}\) respectively; the ratio is \(\sqrt{(1+h_0)/h_0}\). For \(h_0 = 1/398 + (3 - 2.970425)^2/284.705 = 0.002515\): ratio \(= \sqrt{1.002515/0.002515} = 19.96\). The PI at 3000 lb (width 17.10) is about 20 times wider than the CI (width 0.86).

B3 (★★). For \(n = 25\) and \(n = 398\), compare the Working–Hotelling multiplier \(\sqrt{2F_{2,n-2;0.95}}\) with the pointwise multiplier \(t_{n-2;0.975}\). By what percentage is the simultaneous band wider?

Solution

\(n = 25\): \(\sqrt{2F_{2,23;0.95}} = 2.616\) vs \(t_{23;0.975} = 2.069\), about 26% wider. \(n = 398\): \(2.457\) vs \(1.966\), about 25% wider. (R: sqrt(2 * qf(0.95, 2, n - 2)) and qt(0.975, n - 2).)

9.3 C. Computational

C1 (★). Using the Auto MPG fit, compute the 95% CI for the mean and the 95% PI at 2500 lb and at 5000 lb. Which of them involves extrapolation? (Observed weights: 1613–5140 lb.)

Solution

At 2500 lb: fit 27.126, CI (26.636, 27.616), PI (18.570, 35.681). At 5000 lb: fit 7.934, CI (6.821, 9.047), PI (−0.679, 16.548). C2 (★★). Normality matters for prediction. With the 25 design points of the teaching data, simulate 20,000 data sets and new observations at \(x_0 = 3.0\) with (i) normal errors, \(\sigma = 4\), and (ii) errors \(4(E - 1)\), \(E \sim \text{Exp}(1)\) (seed 2026). For the 95% PI, record how often \(y_0\) falls below and above the interval.

Solution (Python) 
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
xt = np.array([2.226, 3.84, 3.235, 2.897, 2.842, 4.367, 4.768, 2.221, 3.885, 2.644, 4.984, 4.819, 3.826,
               4.235, 3.403, 4.491, 3.169, 2.786, 2.573, 2.392, 3.44, 3.108, 3.921, 1.645, 3.167])
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
R, n, x0 = 20_000, len(xt), 3.0
Sxx = np.sum((xt - xt.mean())**2); k = (xt - xt.mean()) / Sxx
q = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 2)
for name, gen in [("normal", lambda s: rng.normal(0, 4, s)),
                  ("skewed", lambda s: 4 * (rng.exponential(1, s) - 1))]:
    Y = 46 - 7.7 * xt + gen((R, n))
    b1 = Y @ k; b0 = Y.mean(axis=1) - b1 * xt.mean()
    s = np.sqrt(((Y - b0[:, None] - b1[:, None] * xt)**2).sum(axis=1) / (n - 2))
    y0 = 46 - 7.7 * x0 + gen(R)
    fit = b0 + b1 * x0
    half = q * s * np.sqrt(1 + 1 / n + (x0 - xt.mean())**2 / Sxx)
    print(name, round((y0 < fit - half).mean(), 4), round((y0 > fit + half).mean(), 4))

Output: normal 0.0252 below, 0.0276 above; skewed 0.0000 below, 0.0598 above. With skewed errors the interval is too long on the left and too short on the right. Remedies include transforming \(y\) or using quantile-based prediction methods.

The log transformation makes the spread more nearly constant (the Breusch–Pagan \(p\)-value on the log scale is 0.09), so coverage becomes more even across weights — the light-car shortfall shrinks from 0.896 to 0.933 — but it is not perfect: the heaviest third is still over-covered.
