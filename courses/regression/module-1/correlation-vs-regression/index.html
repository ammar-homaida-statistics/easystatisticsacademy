1 Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. define the Pearson correlation coefficient \(r\), prove that \(-1 \le r \le 1\), and state when \(|r| = 1\);
  2. show that the least-squares slope equals \(r\,s_y/s_x\) and that, in standardised units, the regression slope is \(r\);
  3. explain why regressing \(y\) on \(x\) and \(x\) on \(y\) give different lines, and show that the product of their slopes is \(r^2\);
  4. explain regression toward the mean and recognise the regression fallacy;
  5. decide when correlation and when regression answers the question being asked, and name the main limitations of \(r\).

2 Motivation

For the Auto MPG data, the correlation between weight and fuel economy is \(r = -0.832\), and the least-squares slope is \(-7.68\) mpg per 1000 lb. Both numbers describe the same scatterplot. Are they two ways of saying the same thing? Not quite. The correlation is symmetric and unit-free; the slope is directional and carries units. This lesson makes the connection exact, shows what each quantity can and cannot tell us, and explains a surprising consequence of \(|r| < 1\): regression toward the mean.

3 Notation for this lesson

Symbol Meaning
\(S_{xx}, S_{yy}, S_{xy}\) corrected sums of squares and cross-products
\(s_x = \sqrt{S_{xx}/(n-1)}\), \(s_y\) sample standard deviations
\(r\) sample (Pearson) correlation coefficient
\(\rho\) population correlation coefficient
\(z_{x,i} = (x_i - \bar x)/s_x\) standardised value of \(x_i\) (similarly \(z_{y,i}\))
\(\hat\beta_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx}\) least-squares slope of \(y\) on \(x\) (Lesson 1.3)
\(\hat\gamma_1 = S_{xy}/S_{yy}\) least-squares slope of \(x\) on \(y\)

All notation follows the course notation standard.

Assumptions used in this lesson

The simple linear regression model is \(y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i\), \(i = 1, \dots, n\), with the \(x_i\) treated as fixed and

  • A1 (Correct mean function). \(E(\varepsilon_i) = 0\) for all \(i\), i.e. \(E(y_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i\).
  • A2 (Constant variance). \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2\) for all \(i\).
  • A3 (Uncorrelated errors). \(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_i, \varepsilon_j) = 0\) for \(i \ne j\).
  • A4 (Normality). \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\); together with A1–A3 this makes the errors independent.

Each result below states which of A1–A4 it uses. Why these assumptions, and what each one is needed for: Lesson 1.1, Assumptions.

4 Theory

4.1 The correlation coefficient

Definition 1.2.1 (Pearson correlation coefficient)

For data \((x_i, y_i)\), \(i = 1,\dots,n\), with \(S_{xx} > 0\) and \(S_{yy} > 0\), \[ r = \frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}\,S_{yy}}} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n z_{x,i}\, z_{y,i}. \]

The second form shows that \(r\) is the average product of standardised values (with divisor \(n-1\)): points in the upper-right and lower-left quadrants around \((\bar x, \bar y)\) push \(r\) up; points in the other two quadrants push it down.

Theorem 1.2.1 (Range of \(r\))

\(-1 \le r \le 1\), and \(|r| = 1\) if and only if all points lie exactly on a straight line with non-zero slope. Moreover, the error sum of squares of the least-squares line of \(y\) on \(x\) is \[ \mathrm{SSE} = S_{yy}\,(1 - r^2). \]

Derivation

With \(\hat\beta_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx}\) and \(\hat y_i - \bar y = \hat\beta_1 (x_i - \bar x)\) (Lesson 1.3), \[ \mathrm{SSE} = \sum \bigl[(y_i - \bar y) - \hat\beta_1 (x_i - \bar x)\bigr]^2 = S_{yy} - 2\hat\beta_1 S_{xy} + \hat\beta_1^2 S_{xx} = S_{yy} - \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}} = S_{yy}\Bigl(1 - \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}S_{yy}}\Bigr) = S_{yy}(1 - r^2). \] Since \(\mathrm{SSE} \ge 0\) and \(S_{yy} > 0\), \(r^2 \le 1\). Equality holds iff \(\mathrm{SSE} = 0\), i.e. every point lies on the fitted line; the slope is then non-zero because \(S_{yy} > 0\). (Equivalently, \(|r| \le 1\) is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the centred vectors \(\mathbf x - \bar x\mathbf 1\) and \(\mathbf y - \bar y\mathbf 1\).)

Theorem 1.2.2 (Invariance)

  1. \(r\) is symmetric: \(r(x, y) = r(y, x)\).
  2. If \(x_i^* = a + b\,x_i\) and \(y_i^* = c + d\,y_i\) with \(b, d \ne 0\), then \(r(x^*, y^*) = \operatorname{sign}(bd)\, r(x, y)\). In particular, \(r\) has no units and does not change when either variable is shifted or rescaled by a positive constant.
Derivation
  1. is immediate from the definition. For (b), \(x_i^* - \bar x^* = b(x_i - \bar x)\) and \(y_i^* - \bar y^* = d(y_i - \bar y)\), so \(S_{x^*y^*} = bd\,S_{xy}\), \(S_{x^*x^*} = b^2 S_{xx}\), \(S_{y^*y^*} = d^2 S_{yy}\), and \(r(x^*, y^*) = bd\,S_{xy}/(|b|\,|d|\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}) = \operatorname{sign}(bd)\,r\).

4.2 Correlation and the least-squares slope

Theorem 1.2.3 (Slope and correlation)

  1. \(\hat\beta_1 = r\,\dfrac{s_y}{s_x}\).
  2. In standardised units the least-squares line of \(z_y\) on \(z_x\) has intercept 0 and slope \(r\): \[ \hat z_y = r\, z_x . \]
Derivation
  1. \(\hat\beta_1 = \dfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \dfrac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}}\sqrt{\dfrac{S_{yy}}{S_{xx}}} = r\,\dfrac{s_y}{s_x}\), since the factors \(n-1\) cancel.
  2. Standardised variables have mean 0 and standard deviation 1, so by Lesson 1.3 the intercept is \(0 - \hat\beta_1\cdot 0 = 0\) and by (a) the slope is \(r \cdot 1/1 = r\); by Theorem 1.2.2 the correlation of \(z_x\) and \(z_y\) is still \(r\).

Theorem 1.2.3 says that the slope and the correlation contain the same information about direction and strength once the scales are fixed. The slope translates \(r\) into the units of the problem: a one-standard-deviation increase in \(x\) is associated with an \(r\)-standard-deviation change in the mean of \(y\).

4.3 Two regression lines

Regressing \(x\) on \(y\) minimises horizontal distances and gives \[ \hat x = \hat\gamma_0 + \hat\gamma_1 y, \qquad \hat\gamma_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{yy}} = r\,\frac{s_x}{s_y}, \qquad \hat\gamma_0 = \bar x - \hat\gamma_1 \bar y . \]

Theorem 1.2.4 (The two regression lines)

  1. Both lines pass through \((\bar x, \bar y)\).
  2. \(\hat\beta_1\,\hat\gamma_1 = r^2\).
  3. Drawn in the same \((x, y)\) plane, the two lines coincide if and only if \(|r| = 1\). When \(0 < |r| < 1\), the \(x\)-on-\(y\) line (slope \(1/\hat\gamma_1\) in that plane) is steeper than the \(y\)-on-\(x\) line.
Derivation
  1. is part (e) of Theorem 1.3.2, Lesson 1.3 applied to each regression. (b) \(\hat\beta_1\hat\gamma_1 = S_{xy}^2/(S_{xx}S_{yy}) = r^2\). (c) In the \((x,y)\) plane the \(x\)-on-\(y\) line has slope \(1/\hat\gamma_1\) (when \(r \ne 0\)). Both lines pass through the same point, so they coincide iff \(\hat\beta_1 = 1/\hat\gamma_1\), i.e. iff \(\hat\beta_1\hat\gamma_1 = r^2 = 1\). If \(0 < r^2 < 1\), then \(|\hat\beta_1| = r^2/|\hat\gamma_1| < 1/|\hat\gamma_1|\). (If \(r = 0\), the \(y\)-on-\(x\) line is horizontal and the \(x\)-on-\(y\) line is vertical.)

The two lines answer different questions: “what is the mean \(y\) for a given \(x\)?” and “what is the mean \(x\) for a given \(y\)?”. Neither is the “true line”. Which one is appropriate depends on which variable is to be predicted.

4.4 Regression toward the mean

From Theorem 1.2.3(b), \(\hat z_y = r\,z_x\). Because \(|r| < 1\) in any real data set, \(|\hat z_y| < |z_x|\) whenever \(z_x \neq 0\): a unit that is extreme in \(x\) is predicted to be less extreme, in standard-deviation units, in \(y\). Francis Galton observed this in 1886 when he found that the children of very tall parents were, on average, tall but less extreme than their parents, and he called it “regression towards mediocrity” — the origin of the word regression.

Regression toward the mean is a property of imperfect correlation, not a causal force. It appears in both directions: the parents of very tall children are also, on average, less extreme than their children.

The regression fallacy

When units are selected because they are extreme on a first measurement, their second measurement will tend to be closer to the mean even if nothing has been done to them. Attributing this change to a treatment, a punishment or a reward is the regression fallacy. A control group selected in the same way is the standard protection against it.

4.5 When to use correlation and when to use regression

Correlation \(r\) Regression slope \(\hat\beta_1\)
Question How strongly are \(x\) and \(y\) linearly associated? How does the mean of \(y\) change with \(x\)?
Symmetry Symmetric in \(x\) and \(y\) Directional: \(y\) on \(x\)
Units None; \(-1 \le r \le 1\) Units of \(y\) per unit of \(x\)
Prediction Not by itself Yes: \(\hat y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x\)
Typical design Both variables random (e.g. bivariate sample) \(x\) fixed or conditioned on

Testing \(H_0\!: \rho = 0\) in a bivariate normal sample and testing \(H_0\!: \beta_1 = 0\) in the regression model give the same \(t\)-statistic; Lesson 1.7 proves this.

4.6 Limitations of \(r\)

  • Only linear association. \(r\) can be zero when \(y\) is a perfect function of \(x\): for \(x = -5, \dots, 5\) and \(y = x^2\), \(r = 0\) exactly (Exercise C.3).
  • Sensitivity to outliers. A single unusual point can create or destroy a large correlation (Module 3).
  • Dependence on the range of \(x\). Under the linear model with \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\) and \(x\) random with variance \(\sigma_x^2\), independent of \(\varepsilon\), \[ \rho^2 = \frac{\beta_1^2\sigma_x^2}{\beta_1^2\sigma_x^2 + \sigma^2}. \] Restricting the range of \(x\) reduces \(\sigma_x^2\) and hence \(\rho^2\), although the slope \(\beta_1\) is unchanged (Exercise B.3). A low correlation in a narrow sample does not mean a weak effect.
  • Association, not causation. As for the slope (Lesson 1.1).

5 Worked example: correlation and the two regression lines for Auto MPG

5.1 Computation

Weight \(x\) is in thousands of pounds and fuel economy \(y\) in mpg, \(n = 398\) (Auto MPG; Quinlan, 1993; CC BY 4.0).

R code 
cols <- c("mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
          "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name")
auto <- read.table("data/auto-mpg.data", col.names = cols, na.strings = "?")
x <- auto$weight / 1000; y <- auto$mpg

r  <- cor(x, y)
b1 <- r * sd(y) / sd(x)                       # Theorem 1.2.3(a)
g1 <- r * sd(x) / sd(y)                       # slope of x on y
c(r = r, sd_x = sd(x), sd_y = sd(y), b1 = b1, g1 = g1,
  product = b1 * g1, r_squared = r^2)

coef(lm(y ~ x))                               # same slope as b1
coef(lm(x ~ y))                               # same slope as g1
cor(auto$weight * 0.45359237, auto$mpg)       # weight in kg: r unchanged
Python code 
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

cols = ["mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
        "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name"]
auto = pd.read_csv("data/auto-mpg.data", sep=r"\s+", header=None,
                   names=cols, na_values="?", quotechar='"')
x = auto["weight"].to_numpy() / 1000
y = auto["mpg"].to_numpy()

r = np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]
sx, sy = x.std(ddof=1), y.std(ddof=1)
b1 = r * sy / sx                               # Theorem 1.2.3(a)
g1 = r * sx / sy                               # slope of x on y
print(f"r = {r:.6f}, s_x = {sx:.6f}, s_y = {sy:.6f}")
print(f"b1 = {b1:.6f}, g1 = {g1:.6f}, b1*g1 = {b1*g1:.6f}, r^2 = {r**2:.6f}")
print("Line of x on y, drawn in the (x, y) plane, has slope", round(1 / g1, 4))
print("r with weight in kg:", round(np.corrcoef(auto["weight"] * 0.45359237, y)[0, 1], 6))

Output 

r = -0.831741, s_x = 0.846842, s_y = 7.815984
b1 = -7.676610, g1 = -0.090117, b1*g1 = 0.691793, r^2 = 0.691793
Line of x on y, drawn in the (x, y) plane, has slope -11.0967
r with weight in kg: -0.831741

Results: \(r = -0.831741\), \(s_x = 0.846842\), \(s_y = 7.815984\). Then

\[ \hat\beta_1 = r\,\frac{s_y}{s_x} = -0.831741 \times \frac{7.815984}{0.846842} = -7.676610, \]

exactly the slope found in Lesson 1.3. The slope of \(x\) on \(y\) is \(\hat\gamma_1 = -0.090117\) thousand lb per mpg, and \(\hat\beta_1\hat\gamma_1 = 0.691793 = r^2\). Measured in kilograms, weight gives the same \(r\) (Theorem 1.2.2).

5.3 Regression toward the mean in the data

Take the heaviest 10% of cars (40 cars). Their mean standardised weight is \(\bar z_x = 1.868\). The straight-line prediction for their mean standardised mpg is \(r\,\bar z_x = -1.553\); their actual mean standardised mpg is \(-1.301\). Both are less extreme than 1.868: very heavy cars are, on average, less extreme in fuel economy than in weight. (The actual value is even closer to the mean than the linear prediction, because the mean function flattens for heavy cars — the curvature seen in Lesson 1.1.)

R code 
# Uses x, y and r from Section 5.1 (run that code first).
zx <- (x - mean(x)) / sd(x); zy <- (y - mean(y)) / sd(y)
heavy <- x >= quantile(x, 0.9)
c(n = sum(heavy), mean_zx = mean(zx[heavy]),
  linear_prediction = r * mean(zx[heavy]), mean_zy = mean(zy[heavy]))
Python code 
# Uses np, x, y, r, sx and sy from Section 5.1 (run that code first).
zx = (x - x.mean()) / sx
zy = (y - y.mean()) / sy
heavy = x >= np.quantile(x, 0.9)
print(f"n = {heavy.sum()}, mean z_x = {zx[heavy].mean():.3f}, "
      f"r * mean z_x = {r * zx[heavy].mean():.3f}, mean z_y = {zy[heavy].mean():.3f}")

Output 

n = 40, mean z_x = 1.868, r * mean z_x = -1.553, mean z_y = -1.301

5.4 Interpretation

  • \(r = -0.832\): a strong negative linear association between weight and fuel economy; \(r^2 = 0.692\) is the proportion of the variation in mpg accounted for by the least-squares line (Lesson 1.6 makes this precise).
  • The slope \(-7.68\) mpg per 1000 lb expresses the same association in the units of the problem, and only in the direction “mpg given weight”.
  • To predict weight from mpg, use the dashed line, not the inverted solid line: inverting \(\hat y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x\) gives a slope of \(1/\hat\beta_1 = -0.130\) thousand lb per mpg, but the least-squares slope of \(x\) on \(y\) is \(-0.090\).

6 Common misconceptions

Misconceptions

  1. “\(r = -0.83\) means 83% of the variation is explained.” — No. The proportion is \(r^2 = 0.69\) (Lesson 1.6), and the sign of \(r\) carries direction only.
  2. “\(r = 0\) means no relationship.” — No. It means no linear association; strong nonlinear relationships can have \(r = 0\).
  3. “Regressing \(x\) on \(y\) is just the inverse of regressing \(y\) on \(x\).” — Only if \(|r| = 1\) (Theorem 1.2.4).
  4. “The improvement of the worst performers shows that the intervention worked.” — Possibly regression toward the mean; a comparable control group is needed.
  5. “A low correlation in a narrow sample means \(x\) has little effect on \(y\).” — Restricting the range of \(x\) lowers \(r\) even when the slope is unchanged.

7 Key takeaways

  • \(r = S_{xy}/\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}\) is symmetric, unit-free and lies in \([-1, 1]\); \(\mathrm{SSE} = S_{yy}(1 - r^2)\).
  • \(\hat\beta_1 = r\,s_y/s_x\); in standardised units the regression slope is \(r\).
  • The regressions of \(y\) on \(x\) and of \(x\) on \(y\) are different lines, with \(\hat\beta_1\hat\gamma_1 = r^2\).
  • Because \(|r| < 1\), predictions regress toward the mean; ignoring this leads to the regression fallacy.
  • Use correlation to summarise linear association, and regression to model and predict a conditional mean.

8 Exercises

8.1 A. Conceptual

A.1 (★). A report states: “The correlation between study hours and exam score was 0.5, so study hours explain 50% of the variation in scores.” Correct the statement.

Solution

The proportion of variation in scores accounted for by a straight-line regression on study hours is \(r^2 = 0.25\), i.e. 25%, not 50%. In addition, “explain” should not be read causally with observational data.

A.2 (★). After a speed-camera programme, a city reports that the 20 junctions with the most accidents last year had 30% fewer accidents this year. Give a reason, other than the cameras, why accidents would fall at these junctions, and say how to check whether the cameras had an effect.

Solution

The junctions were selected because their accident counts were extreme. Counts vary from year to year, so extreme counts are partly due to chance and tend to be followed by values closer to the mean (regression toward the mean), even with no intervention. To estimate the camera effect, compare with similar high-accident junctions that did not receive cameras (a control group selected in the same way), ideally with cameras assigned at random.

A.3 (★★). Explain why a doctor wishing to predict a patient’s weight from height and an engineer wishing to predict height from weight should not use the same fitted line, even with the same data.

Solution

Each wants the conditional mean of a different variable. The least-squares line of weight on height minimises errors in weight; the line of height on weight minimises errors in height. Unless \(|r| = 1\), the two lines differ (Theorem 1.2.4), and inverting one does not give the other.

8.2 B. Mathematical

B.1 (★★). Prove Theorem 1.2.2(b), and deduce that \(r\) is unchanged when weight is converted from pounds to kilograms.

Solution

See the derivation after Theorem 1.2.2. Converting pounds to kilograms is \(x^* = 0.45359237\,x\), i.e. \(a = 0\), \(b = 0.45359237 > 0\); with \(y\) unchanged (\(c = 0\), \(d = 1\)), \(\operatorname{sign}(bd) = +1\) and \(r(x^*, y) = r(x, y)\). The Auto MPG computation confirms \(r = -0.831741\) in both units.

B.2 (★★). Show that, in standardised units, the least-squares line of \(z_x\) on \(z_y\) is \(\hat z_x = r z_y\). Hence show that both standardised regressions “shrink” toward 0, and explain why this means regression toward the mean works in both directions.

Solution

By symmetry of Theorem 1.2.3(b) with the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) exchanged, and \(r(y, x) = r(x, y)\), the line is \(\hat z_x = r z_y\). For \(|r| < 1\), \(|\hat z_x| < |z_y|\) and \(|\hat z_y| < |z_x|\): whichever variable is used to select extreme units, the other variable is predicted to be closer to its mean. Regression toward the mean is therefore a consequence of imperfect correlation, not of time order or causation.

B.3 (★★★). Suppose \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon\) with \(x\) random, \(\operatorname{Var}(x) = \sigma_x^2 > 0\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\), and \(x\) independent of \(\varepsilon\). Show that \[ \rho^2 = \operatorname{Corr}(x, y)^2 = \frac{\beta_1^2\sigma_x^2}{\beta_1^2\sigma_x^2 + \sigma^2}, \] and explain what happens to \(\rho^2\) and to \(\beta_1\) if the population is restricted to a narrower range of \(x\) (assume the linear model still holds there).

Solution

\(\operatorname{Cov}(x, y) = \operatorname{Cov}(x, \beta_1 x + \varepsilon) = \beta_1\sigma_x^2\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(y) = \beta_1^2\sigma_x^2 + \sigma^2\) (independence makes the cross term vanish). Hence \(\rho^2 = \beta_1^2\sigma_x^4 / [\sigma_x^2(\beta_1^2\sigma_x^2 + \sigma^2)] = \beta_1^2\sigma_x^2/(\beta_1^2\sigma_x^2 + \sigma^2)\). Restricting the range of \(x\) reduces \(\sigma_x^2\); with \(\beta_1\) and \(\sigma^2\) unchanged, \(\rho^2\) decreases (it is increasing in \(\sigma_x^2\)). The slope \(\beta_1\) — the change in \(E(y \mid x)\) per unit of \(x\) — is unaffected. Strictly, the conditional distribution of \(\varepsilon\) given the restricted \(x\) is unchanged because \(x\) and \(\varepsilon\) are independent.

C.2 (★★). Simulating regression toward the mean. Generate \(n = 10\,000\) true scores \(T \sim N(100, 15^2)\) and two test scores \(t_1 = T + e_1\), \(t_2 = T + e_2\) with independent errors \(e_1, e_2 \sim N(0, 10^2)\), using seed 2026. Select the top 10% on \(t_1\). Compare their mean \(t_1\) and mean \(t_2\), and compare the mean \(t_2\) with the prediction \(\bar t + r(\bar t_{1,\text{sel}} - \bar t)\). What is the theoretical correlation of \(t_1\) and \(t_2\)?

Hint

\(\operatorname{Cov}(t_1, t_2) = \operatorname{Var}(T)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(t_1) = \operatorname{Var}(T) + \operatorname{Var}(e_1)\).

Solution (Python) 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
n = 10_000
T  = rng.normal(100, 15, n)
t1 = T + rng.normal(0, 10, n)
t2 = T + rng.normal(0, 10, n)
r = np.corrcoef(t1, t2)[0, 1]
sel = t1 >= np.quantile(t1, 0.9)
print(round(r, 4), round(t1[sel].mean(), 2), round(t2[sel].mean(), 2))
print(round(t1.mean() + r * (t1[sel].mean() - t1.mean()), 2))

Theory: \(\rho = 15^2/(15^2 + 10^2) = 225/325 = 0.6923\). With this seed, \(r = 0.6946\); the selected group has mean \(t_1 = 131.51\) but mean \(t_2 = 121.79\), although nothing was done to them, close to the prediction \(100.00 + 0.6946 \times 31.51 \approx 121.9\). (The two tests have equal variances here, so the standardised prediction can be written in raw units. In R, set.seed(2026) gives different draws and slightly different numbers.)

C.3 (★). Compute \(r\) for \(x = -5, -4, \dots, 5\) and \(y = x^2\). Plot the data. What does this show about \(r\)?

Solution

\(r = 0\) exactly: \(S_{xy} = \sum (x_i - 0)(x_i^2 - \bar y) = \sum x_i^3 - \bar y\sum x_i = 0\) because the \(x_i\) are symmetric about 0. Yet \(y\) is an exact function of \(x\). Correlation measures only linear association; always plot the data.
