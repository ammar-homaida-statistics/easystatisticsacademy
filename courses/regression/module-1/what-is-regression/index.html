Lesson 1.1 — What Is Regression?

1 Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. distinguish a functional relationship from a statistical one and define the regression function \(E(y \mid x)\);
  2. prove that \(E(y \mid x)\) is the best predictor of \(y\) from \(x\) under squared-error loss;
  3. write the simple linear regression model, explain each of its components, and say what “linear” means;
  4. state the standard assumptions on the errors and identify which later results need which assumption;
  5. explain why regression on observational data does not, by itself, establish causation.

2 Motivation

The Auto MPG data record fuel economy, weight and other characteristics of 398 cars from model years 1970–1982. Two cars of the same weight can have very different fuel economy, so mpg is not a function of weight. Yet heavier cars clearly tend to have lower mpg. Regression analysis gives a precise meaning to “tend to”: it models how the average response changes with the predictor, and how individual observations scatter around that average.

4 Theory

4.1 Functional and statistical relationships

Definition 1.1.1 (Functional and statistical relationships)

A functional relationship \(y = f(x)\) assigns exactly one value of \(y\) to each value of \(x\); every observation lies on the curve. A statistical relationship is one in which \(y\) varies for a fixed \(x\), but its distribution — in particular its mean — changes with \(x\).

Examples: the price of \(x\) items at a fixed unit price is functional; fuel economy as a function of weight is statistical, because many factors other than weight (engine, gearing, aerodynamics, driving cycle) affect mpg.

4.2 The regression function

Definition 1.1.2 (Regression function)

For random variables \((x, y)\) with \(E(y^2) < \infty\), the regression function of \(y\) on \(x\) is the conditional mean \[ m(x) = E(y \mid x). \] Writing \(\varepsilon = y - m(x)\), every such pair satisfies \[ y = m(x) + \varepsilon, \qquad E(\varepsilon \mid x) = 0 . \]

The decomposition is always available; it is not yet a model. A regression model makes an assumption about the form of \(m(x)\) — and, usually, about the distribution of \(\varepsilon\).

Why the conditional mean? Because it is the best possible predictor under squared-error loss.

Theorem 1.1.1 (Best predictor)

If \(E(y^2) < \infty\), then for every function \(g\) with \(E[g(x)^2] < \infty\), \[ E\bigl[(y - g(x))^2\bigr] = E\bigl[(y - m(x))^2\bigr] + E\bigl[(m(x) - g(x))^2\bigr] \;\ge\; E\bigl[(y - m(x))^2\bigr], \] with equality if and only if \(g(x) = m(x)\) with probability one.

Derivation

Write \(y - g(x) = \bigl(y - m(x)\bigr) + \bigl(m(x) - g(x)\bigr)\) and expand the square. The cross term is \[ 2\,E\bigl[(y - m(x))(m(x) - g(x))\bigr] = 2\,E\Bigl[(m(x) - g(x))\,E\bigl(y - m(x) \mid x\bigr)\Bigr] = 0, \] using the tower property and the fact that \(m(x) - g(x)\) is a function of \(x\), together with \(E(y - m(x) \mid x) = 0\). The second term is non-negative and equals zero exactly when \(g(x) = m(x)\) almost surely.

Theorem 1.1.1 explains the place of squared error in regression: if prediction error is measured by its mean square, the target of estimation is the conditional mean. Lesson 1.3 applies the same loss to a sample.

4.3 The simple linear regression model

Definition 1.1.3 (Simple linear regression model)

For observations \((x_i, y_i)\), \(i = 1, \dots, n\), \[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i , \] where \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) are unknown constants, the \(x_i\) are treated as fixed (or the analysis is conditional on them), and the \(\varepsilon_i\) are random errors with \(E(\varepsilon_i) = 0\).

Each component has a precise meaning:

  • \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\) is the assumed regression function \(m(x)\); the model claims the conditional mean is a straight line in \(x\).
  • \(\beta_1\) is the change in the mean response for a one-unit increase in \(x\): \(m(x+1) - m(x) = \beta_1\).
  • \(\beta_0 = m(0)\) is the mean response at \(x = 0\), meaningful only if \(x = 0\) is a sensible value (Lesson 1.5).
  • \(\varepsilon_i\) collects everything that makes \(y_i\) differ from its mean: omitted factors, measurement error, inherent variability.

Remark: what “linear” means

“Linear” refers to the parameters, not to \(x\). The model \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \varepsilon\) is a linear model, and so is \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log x + \varepsilon\). The model \(y = \beta_0 + e^{\beta_1 x} + \varepsilon\) is not, because it is not linear in \(\beta_1\).

4.4 Assumptions and what they are used for

Assumptions of the simple linear regression model

  • A1 (Correct mean function). \(E(\varepsilon_i) = 0\) for all \(i\), i.e. \(E(y_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i\).
  • A2 (Constant variance). \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2\) for all \(i\).
  • A3 (Uncorrelated errors). \(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_i, \varepsilon_j) = 0\) for \(i \ne j\).
  • A4 (Normality). \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\); together with A1–A3 this makes the errors independent.

Not every result needs every assumption. This table is the map for Module 1:

Result Needs Lesson
Computing the OLS estimates and the fitted-line identities none (only \(S_{xx} > 0\)) 1.3
Unbiasedness of \(\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1\) A1 1.4
Variance formulas; Gauss–Markov (BLUE) A1–A3 1.4
Unbiasedness of \(\hat\sigma^2\) A1–A3 1.4
Exact \(t\) and \(F\) tests; exact confidence and prediction intervals A1–A4 1.7–1.8

Two clarifications prevent common errors. First, A4 concerns the errors, not the marginal distribution of \(y\) or \(x\): \(y\) is not identically distributed under the model, because its mean depends on \(x_i\). Second, A1–A4 are statements about the data-generating process; they cannot be “proved” from data, only checked for gross violations (Module 3).

4.5 Purposes of regression

Regression is used for three related but distinct purposes:

  • Description — summarising how the mean of \(y\) varies with \(x\) in the observed data.
  • Prediction — predicting \(y\) for new units with known \(x\) (Lesson 1.8).
  • Estimating effects — estimating how \(y\) would change if \(x\) were changed. This is the most demanding purpose and depends on how the data were collected.

4.6 Observational versus experimental data

In a designed experiment, the investigator assigns values of \(x\) to units, ideally at random. Randomisation balances, on average, all other factors across levels of \(x\), so a difference in mean response can be attributed to \(x\).

In observational data, \(x\) is merely recorded. Units with different \(x\) may differ systematically in other ways (confounding), so \(\beta_1\) describes an association, not necessarily the effect of changing \(x\). The Auto MPG data are observational: heavier cars also tend to have larger engines (the correlation between weight and engine displacement is 0.933 in these data), so the association between weight and mpg mixes the role of weight with that of engine size.

5.1 The data

5.2 Looking at the conditional mean

The regression function \(E(\text{mpg} \mid \text{weight})\) is unknown, but we can estimate it without assuming any form by averaging mpg within narrow weight bands. If the band means lie close to a straight line, the linear model (Definition 1.1.3) is a reasonable description.

R code 
cols <- c("mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
          "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name")
auto <- read.table("data/auto-mpg.data", col.names = cols, na.strings = "?")

summary(auto[, c("mpg", "weight")])

# Mean mpg within 500-lb weight bands (the heaviest band is 4500-5500 lb)
breaks <- c(1500, 2000, 2500, 3000, 3500, 4000, 4500, 5500)
auto$band <- cut(auto$weight, breaks = breaks, right = FALSE, dig.lab = 4)
band_stats <- aggregate(mpg ~ band, data = auto,
                        FUN = function(v) c(n = length(v), mean = mean(v), sd = sd(v)))
band_stats <- do.call(data.frame, band_stats)
names(band_stats) <- c("band", "n", "mean_mpg", "sd_mpg")
print(band_stats, digits = 4)
Python code 
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

cols = ["mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
        "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name"]
auto = pd.read_csv("data/auto-mpg.data", sep=r"\s+", header=None,
                   names=cols, na_values="?", quotechar='"')

print(auto[["mpg", "weight"]].describe().round(2))

# Mean mpg within 500-lb weight bands (the heaviest band is 4500-5500 lb)
breaks = [1500, 2000, 2500, 3000, 3500, 4000, 4500, 5500]
auto["band"] = pd.cut(auto["weight"], bins=breaks, right=False)
band_stats = (auto.groupby("band", observed=True)["mpg"]
                  .agg(n="count", mean_mpg="mean", sd_mpg="std").round(2))
print(band_stats)

5.3 Interpretation

  • A clear statistical relationship. Mean mpg falls steadily from 33.2 in the lightest band to 12.4 in the heaviest, while cars of similar weight still differ by many mpg.
  • Close to linear, but not exactly. The drop between consecutive bands is largest in the middle (about 5 mpg per 500 lb between 2000 and 3500 lb) and smaller at the heavy end (about 2–3 mpg per 500 lb). The conditional mean is slightly curved. A straight line is a useful first approximation, and Lessons 1.3–1.6 use it; Module 3 shows how to detect and model the curvature.
  • Non-constant spread. The standard deviation of mpg within bands falls from about 4.5–6 mpg for light cars to about 1.6 mpg for the heaviest. Assumption A2 is therefore doubtful here; Lessons 1.7–1.8 show why this matters for inference and prediction intervals.
  • Association, not causation. These are observational data, and weight is strongly correlated with engine size. The relationship describes how mpg varies with weight across these cars; it does not say how much a given car’s mpg would change if weight were added to it.

6 Common misconceptions

Misconceptions

  1. “Linear regression can only fit straight lines.” — No. “Linear” means linear in the parameters; polynomials and transformed predictors are linear models.
  2. “The response must be normally distributed.” — No. The normality assumption (A4), when it is needed, concerns the errors. The marginal distribution of \(y\) is a mixture over the \(x\) values and need not be normal. The predictor need not be normal either.
  3. “A strong regression relationship shows that \(x\) causes \(y\).” — Not from observational data. Causal conclusions require a design (randomisation) or additional assumptions and methods.
  4. “The error and the residual are the same thing.” — No. The error \(\varepsilon_i = y_i - (\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i)\) involves the unknown true parameters; the residual \(e_i\) (Lesson 1.3) uses the estimates.

7 Key takeaways

  • Regression models the conditional mean \(E(y \mid x)\) and the scatter of \(y\) around it.
  • The conditional mean is the best predictor of \(y\) under squared-error loss (Theorem 1.1.1).
  • The simple linear regression model assumes \(E(y \mid x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\); “linear” means linear in the parameters.
  • Assumptions A1–A4 are needed for different results; computing the least-squares line needs none of them.
  • With observational data, regression describes associations; causal interpretation needs more.

8 Exercises

8.1 A. Conceptual

A1 (★). Give one example of a functional relationship and one of a statistical relationship from your own field. For the statistical one, name two factors that make \(y\) vary at a fixed \(x\).

Solution

Answers vary. Example: the area of a square as a function of its side length is functional. A patient’s systolic blood pressure as a function of age is statistical: diet, medication and genetics make blood pressure vary among patients of the same age.

A2 (★). Which of the following are linear regression models (in the sense of this lesson)? For any that are not, say whether a transformation makes them linear.

  1. \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \varepsilon\) (ii) \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log x + \varepsilon\) (iii) \(y = \beta_0 e^{\beta_1 x}\,\delta\) with a positive multiplicative error \(\delta\) (iv) \(y = \beta_0 + e^{\beta_1 x} + \varepsilon\) (v) \(y = \beta_0 / (1 + \beta_1 x) + \varepsilon\)
Solution
  1. Linear (in \(\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2\)). (ii) Linear. (iii) Not linear as written, but taking logs gives \(\log y = \log\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \log\delta\), a linear model for \(\log y\) with intercept \(\log\beta_0\). (iv) Not linear in \(\beta_1\), and no transformation of \(y\) makes it linear because the error is additive. (v) Not linear in \(\beta_1\); with an additive error it is a nonlinear regression model.

A3 (★★). A newspaper reports: “Analysis of 398 cars shows that every extra 1000 lb of weight costs about 7.7 mpg, so manufacturers can gain 7.7 mpg by removing 1000 lb.” Identify two problems with the conclusion.

Solution
  1. The data are observational: heavier cars also have larger engines (weight–displacement correlation 0.933), more cylinders and different designs, so the slope mixes the association of mpg with weight and with these confounded factors. Removing weight from a given car would not necessarily change mpg by the same amount. (2) The relationship is not exactly linear (band means flatten for heavy cars), so a single slope does not describe the change everywhere; and removing 1000 lb from a light car may take it outside the range of the data (extrapolation).

8.2 B. Mathematical

B1 (★). Under the simple linear regression model with A1–A3, find \(E(y_i)\), \(\operatorname{Var}(y_i)\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(y_i, y_j)\) for \(i \ne j\). Are the \(y_i\) identically distributed?

Solution

Since \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i\) is a constant: \(E(y_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i\), \(\operatorname{Var}(y_i) = \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2\), and \(\operatorname{Cov}(y_i, y_j) = \operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_i, \varepsilon_j) = 0\). The \(y_i\) are not identically distributed unless \(\beta_1 = 0\) or all \(x_i\) are equal, because their means differ.

B2 (★★). Let \(y = m(x) + \varepsilon\) with \(m(x) = E(y \mid x)\) and \(E(y^2) < \infty\). Show that \(E(\varepsilon) = 0\) and that \(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon, g(x)) = 0\) for every function \(g\) with \(E[g(x)^2] < \infty\). Conclude that \(\varepsilon\) is uncorrelated with \(x\) (when \(E(x^2) < \infty\)).

Solution

\(E(\varepsilon \mid x) = E(y \mid x) - m(x) = 0\), so \(E(\varepsilon) = E[E(\varepsilon \mid x)] = 0\). Then \(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon, g(x)) = E[\varepsilon\, g(x)] = E[g(x)\, E(\varepsilon \mid x)] = 0\). Taking \(g(x) = x\) gives \(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon, x) = 0\). Note the converse fails: zero correlation with \(x\) does not imply \(E(\varepsilon \mid x) = 0\).

B3 (★★★). Prove Theorem 1.1.1 in detail, stating where \(E(y^2) < \infty\) and \(E[g(x)^2] < \infty\) are used.

Solution

The moment conditions guarantee that all expectations below are finite: \(E[(y - g(x))^2] \le 2E(y^2) + 2E[g(x)^2] < \infty\), and \(E[m(x)^2] \le E(y^2)\) by Jensen’s inequality for conditional expectations, so \(m(x) - g(x)\) has finite second moment and, by Cauchy–Schwarz, the cross term is finite. Then expand \(E[(y - g(x))^2] = E[(y - m(x))^2] + 2E[(y - m(x))(m(x) - g(x))] + E[(m(x) - g(x))^2]\). For the cross term, condition on \(x\): \(E[(y - m(x))(m(x) - g(x)) \mid x] = (m(x) - g(x))\,E(y - m(x) \mid x) = 0\), so its expectation is zero. The last term is \(\ge 0\), with equality iff \(m(x) = g(x)\) almost surely.

8.3 C. Computational

C1 (★). For the Auto MPG data, compute the number of cars and the mean mpg for each number of cylinders. Interpret the result as an estimate of \(E(\text{mpg} \mid \text{cylinders})\). Which estimates are unreliable, and why?

Solution

In R: aggregate(mpg ~ cylinders, data = auto, FUN = function(v) c(n = length(v), mean = mean(v))). In Python: auto.groupby("cylinders")["mpg"].agg(["count", "mean"]).

Cylinders 3 4 5 6 8
Cars 4 204 3 84 103
Mean mpg 20.55 29.29 27.37 19.99 14.96

Each mean estimates the conditional mean of mpg for cars with that number of cylinders, with no assumption about the form of the relationship. Mean mpg falls from 4 to 6 to 8 cylinders. The 3- and 5-cylinder means rest on only 4 and 3 cars and are unreliable; they should not be read as evidence that 3-cylinder cars are less economical than 4-cylinder cars.

C2 (★★). Simulate \(n = 200\) observations from \(y_i = 46 - 7.7\,x_i + \varepsilon_i\) with \(x_i \sim \text{Uniform}(1.6, 5.1)\) (weight in 1000 lb) and \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 4^2)\), using a fixed seed. Plot the data with the true regression line, and compute band means as in the worked example. How do the simulated band means and spreads differ from those of the real data?

Solution (Python) 
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
n = 200
x = rng.uniform(1.6, 5.1, n)
y = 46 - 7.7 * x + rng.normal(0, 4, n)
bands = pd.cut(x, bins=[1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 5.5], right=False)
print(pd.DataFrame({"y": y, "band": bands}).groupby("band", observed=True)["y"]
        .agg(["count", "mean", "std"]).round(2))

In simulated data the band means lie close to the straight line \(46 - 7.7x\) (up to sampling error), and the within-band standard deviations are all near 4, because the data were generated with a linear mean and constant variance. In the real data the band means flatten for heavy cars and the spread shrinks from about 6 to about 1.6 mpg: evidence of curvature and non-constant variance. (The exact simulated numbers depend on the random-number generator; in R use set.seed(2026) and runif/rnorm, which give different draws from Python.)
