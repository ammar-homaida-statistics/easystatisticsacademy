1 Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. write the multiple linear regression model and state what each coefficient means as a partial association;
  2. explain why the slope of a predictor changes when another predictor is added, and when it does not;
  3. prove and use the identity that splits a simple-regression slope into a partial slope plus an indirect part;
  4. interpret the coefficients of a fitted model with two predictors correctly, including a centred intercept;
  5. recognise hidden extrapolation: a combination of predictor values outside the data even though each value is inside its own range.

2 Motivation

Lesson 1.9 ended with a list of things a single predictor cannot do. One of them is now pressing. In the Auto MPG data, heavier cars tend to be older (correlation between weight and model year: \(-0.307\)), and newer cars are more fuel-efficient at any weight, partly because of fuel-economy standards introduced in the 1970s. The simple slope of \(-7.68\) mpg per 1000 lb therefore mixes two things: the association of weight with fuel economy, and the association of model year with fuel economy that “travels with” weight. Multiple regression separates them. When model year is added, the weight slope becomes \(-6.66\) mpg per 1000 lb, and \(R^2\) rises from \(0.692\) to \(0.808\). This lesson explains exactly what the new number means and where the difference of about \(1.01\) mpg went.

3 Notation for this lesson

Symbol Meaning
\(p\) number of predictors; the model has \(p + 1\) coefficients
\(x_{ij}\) value of predictor \(j\) for unit \(i\) (\(i = 1,\dots,n\); \(j = 1,\dots,p\))
\(\beta_j\), \(\hat\beta_j\) partial regression coefficient of \(x_j\) and its least-squares estimate
\(\tilde\beta_1\) slope of the simple regression of \(y\) on \(x_1\) alone (marginal slope)
\(\hat\delta\) slope of the simple regression of \(x_2\) on \(x_1\)
\(S_{jk} = \sum_i (x_{ij}-\bar x_j)(x_{ik}-\bar x_k)\) centred cross-products; \(S_{jy}\) likewise with \(y\)

All notation follows the course notation standard. Matrix notation starts in Lesson 2.2.

Assumptions used in this lesson

The multiple linear regression model is \(y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \dots + \beta_p x_{ip} + \varepsilon_i\), \(i = 1, \dots, n\), with the predictors treated as fixed and the same assumptions on the errors as in Module 1:

  • A1 (Correct mean function). \(E(\varepsilon_i) = 0\) for all \(i\), i.e. \(E(y_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \dots + \beta_p x_{ip}\).
  • A2 (Constant variance). \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2\) for all \(i\).
  • A3 (Uncorrelated errors). \(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_i, \varepsilon_j) = 0\) for \(i \ne j\).
  • A4 (Normality). \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\); together with A1–A3 this makes the errors independent.

Full discussion: Lesson 1.1, Assumptions. Lesson 2.2 restates them in matrix form and adds the full-rank condition A0.

4 Theory

4.1 The model

The multiple linear regression model with \(p\) predictors is \[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \dots + \beta_p x_{ip} + \varepsilon_i, \qquad i = 1,\dots,n, \] with the same assumptions on the errors as in Module 1: \(E(\varepsilon_i) = 0\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2\), uncorrelated errors, and (for exact inference) normality. The predictors are treated as fixed. “Linear” refers to linearity in the coefficients: \(x_{i2}\) may be \(x_{i1}^2\), \(\log x_{i1}\) or an indicator (Lessons 2.9–2.11).

The mean function \(E(y \mid x_1,\dots,x_p) = \beta_0 + \sum_j \beta_j x_j\) is a plane when \(p = 2\) and a hyperplane in general. The least-squares estimates minimise \[ \mathrm{SSE}(b_0,\dots,b_p) = \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(y_i - b_0 - b_1x_{i1} - \dots - b_px_{ip}\bigr)^2 , \] and \(\hat\sigma^2 = \mathrm{SSE}/(n-p-1)\): one degree of freedom is lost for each estimated coefficient. \(R^2 = 1 - \mathrm{SSE}/\mathrm{SST}\) as before. Lessons 2.2–2.4 derive the estimator and its properties in matrix form; here we concentrate on what the coefficients mean.

4.2 What a partial coefficient means

For \(j = 1\), say, \[ \beta_1 = E(y \mid x_1 + 1, x_2, \dots, x_p) - E(y \mid x_1, x_2, \dots, x_p) \] for every fixed value of \(x_2,\dots,x_p\). So \(\beta_1\) compares the mean response of units that differ by one unit in \(x_1\) and have the same values of all the other predictors in the model. This is a partial (or adjusted, conditional) association.

Template for a correct partial-slope statement

“Among [population/sample], for units with the same [list of other predictors], a difference of [one unit, with unit name] in [\(x_j\)] is associated with a difference of [\(\hat\beta_j\), with unit name] in the average [y].”

Three conditions are hidden in this sentence.

  • The comparison set depends on the model. “Holding the others fixed” refers only to the predictors in the model. The coefficient of weight in a model with model year answers a different question from the coefficient of weight alone, or with horsepower. Neither is “the” effect of weight.
  • Holding fixed is statistical, not experimental. With observational data nobody held anything fixed; the model adjusts by comparing units that differ in \(x_j\) at given values of the others, and relies on the linear form to do so. Variables not in the model are not held fixed.
  • The comparison must be possible. If \(x_j\) cannot change while the others stay fixed (e.g. \(x\) and \(x^2\), or a variable and an interaction containing it), \(\beta_j\) alone has no “one unit, others fixed” meaning (Lessons 2.10–2.11).

The intercept is \(E(y \mid x_1 = \dots = x_p = 0)\). It is meaningful only if the point where all predictors are zero is meaningful and lies near the data; centring each predictor at a typical value makes the intercept the estimated mean response of a typical unit.

4.3 Marginal versus partial slopes

The simple regression of \(y\) on \(x_1\) estimates the slope of \(E(y \mid x_1)\), in which the other predictors are not held fixed: they vary with \(x_1\) as they do in the data. The two slopes are linked by an exact identity.

Theorem 2.1.1 (Marginal slope = partial slope + indirect part)

Let \((\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1, \hat\beta_2)\) be the least-squares estimates for \(y\) on \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), let \(\tilde\beta_1 = S_{1y}/S_{11}\) be the simple-regression slope of \(y\) on \(x_1\), and let \(\hat\delta = S_{12}/S_{11}\) be the simple-regression slope of \(x_2\) on \(x_1\). Then \[ \tilde\beta_1 = \hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_2\,\hat\delta . \] In particular, \(\tilde\beta_1 = \hat\beta_1\) if and only if \(\hat\beta_2 = 0\) or \(S_{12} = 0\) (the predictors are uncorrelated in the sample).

Derivation

Write \(y_i = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_{i1} + \hat\beta_2 x_{i2} + e_i\), where \(e_i\) are the residuals of the two-predictor fit. Setting the partial derivatives of SSE with respect to \(b_0\) and \(b_1\) to zero gives \(\sum_i e_i = 0\) and \(\sum_i x_{i1} e_i = 0\), hence \(\sum_i (x_{i1} - \bar x_1)e_i = 0\).

Multiply \(y_i - \bar y = \hat\beta_1(x_{i1} - \bar x_1) + \hat\beta_2(x_{i2} - \bar x_2) + e_i\) (the centred form, using \(\bar e = 0\)) by \(x_{i1} - \bar x_1\) and sum: \[ S_{1y} = \hat\beta_1 S_{11} + \hat\beta_2 S_{12} + 0 . \] Dividing by \(S_{11}\) gives \(\tilde\beta_1 = \hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_2 S_{12}/S_{11} = \hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_2\hat\delta\). Since \(S_{11} > 0\), the indirect part vanishes iff \(\hat\beta_2 = 0\) or \(S_{12} = 0\).

The identity is algebraic: it holds in every data set, whatever the true model. The indirect part \(\hat\beta_2\hat\delta\) is the part of the marginal slope that comes from \(x_2\): units with larger \(x_1\) tend to have \(x_2\) different by \(\hat\delta\) per unit, and each unit of \(x_2\) goes with \(\hat\beta_2\) in the mean response. Lesson 2.5 gives the population version, the omitted-variable bias formula, and its matrix generalisation.

Two consequences are worth stating at once.

  • A marginal and a partial slope can differ in size and even in sign (Exercise B.3).
  • Adding a predictor that is uncorrelated with \(x_1\) in the sample leaves \(\hat\beta_1\) unchanged (it can still change its standard error, through \(\hat\sigma\)).

4.4 Hidden extrapolation

With one predictor, extrapolation means going outside the observed range of \(x\). With several predictors the data occupy a region of the predictor space, usually much smaller than the box formed by the separate ranges. A point can lie inside every separate range and still be far from the data. Predictions and interpretations there rest on the linear form alone. Module 3 gives a formal tool (leverage, \(h_{00} = \mathbf{x}_0^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_0\)); for now, plot the predictors against each other.

5 Worked example

5.1 Three models for fuel economy

We fit mpg on weight (1000 lb), on model year (years since 1900, as recorded), and on both, for all \(n = 398\) cars.

Model \(\hat\beta_0\) Weight (per 1000 lb) Model year (per year) \(R^2\) \(\hat\sigma\)
Weight only 46.317 −7.677 (0.257) — 0.692 4.345
Year only −69.556 — 1.224 (0.087) 0.336 6.379
Weight + year −14.198 −6.664 (0.214) 0.757 (0.049) 0.808 3.435

Standard errors in parentheses; their derivation comes in Lesson 2.4.

Interpretation.

  • Weight: among cars of the same model year, a difference of 1000 lb is associated with a difference of \(-6.66\) mpg in average fuel economy.
  • Model year: among cars of the same weight, cars one model year newer have, on average, \(0.76\) mpg higher fuel economy.
  • Intercept: \(-14.2\) mpg is the fitted value for a weightless car built in 1900, a meaningless extrapolation. With weight centred at 3000 lb and year at 1976, the intercept becomes \(23.31\) mpg (se \(0.172\)): the estimated mean fuel economy of a 3000-lb car from 1976. The slopes do not change.

5.2 Where did the 1.01 mpg go?

Regressing model year on weight gives \(\hat\delta = -1.339\) years per 1000 lb: heavier cars in the data are, on average, older. Theorem 2.1.1 then gives \[ \underbrace{-7.677}_{\tilde\beta_1\ \text{(weight only)}} \;=\; \underbrace{-6.664}_{\hat\beta_1\ \text{(weight, year fixed)}} \;+\; \underbrace{0.757}_{\hat\beta_2} \times \underbrace{(-1.339)}_{\hat\delta} \;=\; -6.664 - 1.013 . \] About \(1.01\) mpg of the simple slope (13%) reflects the fact that heavier cars tend to be older, and older cars are less economical at the same weight. The same identity, with the roles of the two predictors exchanged, explains why the year slope falls from \(1.224\) to \(0.757\): \(1.224 = 0.757 + (-6.664)(-0.0702)\), where \(-0.0702\) thousand lb per year is the slope of weight on year.

R code 
cols <- c("mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
          "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name")
auto <- read.table("data/auto-mpg.data", col.names = cols, na.strings = "?")
auto$w <- auto$weight / 1000                                   # 1000 lb

m_w  <- lm(mpg ~ w, data = auto)
m_yr <- lm(mpg ~ model_year, data = auto)
m_2  <- lm(mpg ~ w + model_year, data = auto)
summary(m_2)$coefficients
c(R2_w = summary(m_w)$r.squared, R2_yr = summary(m_yr)$r.squared,
  R2_both = summary(m_2)$r.squared, sigma_both = sigma(m_2))

# Centred intercept: mean mpg of a 3000-lb car from 1976
coef(lm(mpg ~ I(w - 3) + I(model_year - 76), data = auto))

# Theorem 2.1.1
delta <- unname(coef(lm(model_year ~ w, data = auto))[2])
b <- unname(coef(m_2))
c(simple_slope = unname(coef(m_w)[2]), partial = b[2], beta2_times_delta = b[3] * delta,
  partial_plus_indirect = b[2] + b[3] * delta, delta = delta)
Python code 
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

cols = ["mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
        "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name"]
auto = pd.read_csv("data/auto-mpg.data", sep=r"\s+", header=None,
                   names=cols, na_values="?", quotechar='"')
y = auto["mpg"].to_numpy()
w = auto["weight"].to_numpy() / 1000                            # 1000 lb
yr = auto["model_year"].to_numpy().astype(float)

def ls_fit(y, *xs):
    """Least squares with an intercept; the matrix formulas are derived in Lessons 2.3-2.4."""
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), *xs])
    XtX_inv = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
    b = XtX_inv @ X.T @ y
    e = y - X @ b
    n, k = X.shape
    s2 = e @ e / (n - k)
    return dict(b=b, se=np.sqrt(s2 * np.diag(XtX_inv)), sigma=np.sqrt(s2),
                R2=1 - e @ e / np.sum((y - y.mean())**2))

for name, xs in {"weight only": (w,), "year only": (yr,), "weight + year": (w, yr)}.items():
    f = ls_fit(y, *xs)
    print(f"{name:14s} b = {np.round(f['b'], 3)}  se = {np.round(f['se'], 3)}  "
          f"R2 = {f['R2']:.3f}  sigma = {f['sigma']:.3f}")

fc = ls_fit(y, w - 3, yr - 76)
print(f"centred intercept = {fc['b'][0]:.3f} (se {fc['se'][0]:.3f})")

# Theorem 2.1.1
b = ls_fit(y, w, yr)["b"]
delta = ls_fit(yr, w)["b"][1]
print(f"simple slope {ls_fit(y, w)['b'][1]:.4f} = partial {b[1]:.4f} + "
      f"{b[2]:.4f} x {delta:.4f} = {b[1] + b[2] * delta:.4f}")

5.4 Hidden extrapolation

The separate ranges are 1613–5140 lb for weight and 1970–1982 for model year. A 4500-lb car from 1982 lies inside both ranges, and the model predicts \(17.9\) mpg for it. But the heaviest 1982 car in the data weighs 3035 lb, and only one car from 1980–1982 weighs more than 3500 lb: heavy cars disappeared from the later model years. The prediction is an extrapolation into an empty part of the (weight, year) plane, supported only by the assumption that the plane extends there.

5.5 Interpretation in context

Among the 398 cars in the Auto MPG data (model years 1970–1982), cars of the same model year that differ by 1000 lb in weight differ on average by about 6.7 mpg in fuel economy; without adjustment for model year the difference is 7.7 mpg, because heavier cars in these data tend to be older. Weight and model year together account for 81% of the variation in fuel economy. These are adjusted associations in observational data, valid within the region of weights and years actually observed, and the model still ignores the curvature seen in Lesson 1.9.

6 Common misconceptions

Misconceptions

  1. “The coefficient of weight is the effect of weight on mpg.” — It is the association adjusted for the other predictors in the model. Variables left out are not held fixed, and the data are observational.
  2. “If the model is correct, adding a predictor should not change the other coefficients.” — The estimates change whenever the added predictor is correlated with the existing ones and has a non-zero coefficient (Theorem 2.1.1). The two coefficients answer different questions.
  3. “The simple slope was wrong.” — It correctly describes \(E(y \mid x_1)\), the average difference between cars that differ in weight, whatever their year. Which slope is relevant depends on the question.
  4. “If each predictor value is inside its observed range, there is no extrapolation.” — The relevant region is the joint one (hidden extrapolation).
  5. “Higher \(R^2\) shows the adjusted coefficients are causal.” — \(R^2\) measures fit, not the validity of a causal interpretation.

7 Key takeaways

  • In multiple regression, \(\beta_j\) is the difference in the mean response per unit of \(x_j\) among units with the same values of the other predictors in the model.
  • Marginal and partial slopes differ by an indirect part: \(\tilde\beta_1 = \hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_2\hat\delta\), exactly, in every sample.
  • Adding a predictor leaves a slope unchanged only if the predictors are uncorrelated in the sample or the new coefficient is zero.
  • Centring all predictors makes the intercept the mean response of a typical unit.
  • Check the joint region of the predictors before predicting or interpreting: hidden extrapolation is invisible in the separate ranges.

8 Exercises

8.1 A. Conceptual

A.1 (★). For the fit \(\widehat{\text{mpg}} = -14.20 - 6.66\,\text{weight (1000 lb)} + 0.757\,\text{model year}\), say whether each statement is correct, and fix the incorrect ones. (a) “Among cars of the same model year, heavier cars have lower fuel economy on average, by about 6.7 mpg per 1000 lb.” (b) “If a manufacturer reduces a car’s weight by 1000 lb, its fuel economy will rise by 6.66 mpg.” (c) “Because the weight slope changed from −7.68 to −6.66 when model year was added, the simple regression was biased and should not be reported.” (d) “The model predicts −14.20 mpg for a car with no weight.”

Solution
  1. Correct. (b) Incorrect: a causal claim about one car from observational data; correct form is (a). (c) Incorrect: the simple slope correctly describes the unadjusted average difference; it answers a different question. Whether it is “biased” depends on which quantity one wants to estimate (Lesson 2.5). (d) Only formally: the fitted value at weight 0 and model year 0 (1900) is a meaningless extrapolation; the intercept only positions the plane.

A.2 (★★). A study regresses annual salary on years of work experience and age for employees aged 25–60. Explain what the coefficient of experience means. Why might it be estimated imprecisely?

Solution

It is the difference in average salary per extra year of experience among employees of the same age. Experience and age are strongly correlated, so among people of the same age the variation in experience is limited (it reflects, e.g., later career starts or career breaks). The data contain little information about this comparison, and the standard error is large. Lesson 2.5 makes this precise: \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j)\) grows by the factor \(1/(1 - R_j^2)\), the variance inflation factor.

8.2 B. Mathematical

B.1 (★★). Show that, with the roles of the predictors exchanged, \(\tilde\beta_2 = \hat\beta_2 + \hat\beta_1\hat\gamma\), where \(\tilde\beta_2\) is the slope of \(y\) on \(x_2\) and \(\hat\gamma = S_{12}/S_{22}\) is the slope of \(x_1\) on \(x_2\). Verify numerically for model year in the worked example.

Solution

The residuals satisfy \(\sum_i (x_{i2} - \bar x_2)e_i = 0\) (normal equation for \(b_2\)). Multiplying the centred form by \(x_{i2} - \bar x_2\) and summing gives \(S_{2y} = \hat\beta_1 S_{12} + \hat\beta_2 S_{22}\); divide by \(S_{22}\). Numerically: \(\hat\gamma = -0.07021\) thousand lb per year, and \(0.75657 + (-6.66388)(-0.07021) = 0.75657 + 0.46787 = 1.22444\), the simple slope on year.

B.2 (★★). Using the two equations \(S_{1y} = \hat\beta_1 S_{11} + \hat\beta_2 S_{12}\) and \(S_{2y} = \hat\beta_1 S_{12} + \hat\beta_2 S_{22}\), solve for \(\hat\beta_1\) and show that \[ \hat\beta_1 = \frac{S_{22}S_{1y} - S_{12}S_{2y}}{S_{11}S_{22} - S_{12}^2} . \] When is the denominator zero, and what does that mean?

Solution

Multiply the first equation by \(S_{22}\), the second by \(S_{12}\), and subtract: \(S_{22}S_{1y} - S_{12}S_{2y} = \hat\beta_1(S_{11}S_{22} - S_{12}^2)\). The denominator equals \(S_{11}S_{22}(1 - r_{12}^2)\), which is zero iff \(|r_{12}| = 1\) (or one predictor is constant), i.e. \(x_2\) is an exact linear function of \(x_1\). Then the two slopes cannot be separated: infinitely many \((\hat\beta_1, \hat\beta_2)\) give the same fit. This is perfect collinearity (full-rank condition, Lesson 2.3); near-collinearity makes the denominator small and the estimates unstable (Module 5).

B.3 (★★). Construct a small numerical example (or describe a mechanism) in which the simple slope of \(y\) on \(x_1\) is positive but the partial slope given \(x_2\) is negative. Use Theorem 2.1.1 to state the condition on \(\hat\beta_2\) and \(\hat\delta\).

Solution

Reversal requires \(\hat\beta_1 < 0 < \tilde\beta_1 = \hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_2\hat\delta\), i.e. \(\hat\beta_2\hat\delta > |\hat\beta_1|\): \(x_2\) must be strongly related to both \(y\) and \(x_1\), with signs such that the product is positive and large. Example: \(x_1\) = hours of study, \(x_2\) = prior weakness in the subject, \(y\) = time to finish an exam. Weaker students study more (\(\hat\delta > 0\)) and need more time (\(\hat\beta_2 > 0\)); among students with the same prior weakness, more study is associated with less time (\(\hat\beta_1 < 0\)), but marginally the association can be positive. A numerical version: \(x_1 = (1, 2, 3, 4)\), \(x_2 = (0, 1.2, 1.9, 3.1)\) and \(y = 5 - x_1 + 3x_2\) (no error term). The two-predictor fit recovers \(\hat\beta_1 = -1\), \(\hat\beta_2 = 3\) exactly; \(\hat\delta = 1.0\), so \(\tilde\beta_1 = -1 + 3 \times 1.0 = 2.0 > 0\). (\(x_2\) must not be an exact linear function of \(x_1\), or the partial slopes cannot be separated; Exercise B.2.)

8.3 C. Computational

C.1 (★). Fit mpg on acceleration (seconds from 0 to 60 mph; larger = slower) alone and then with weight. Report both acceleration slopes, explain the change using Theorem 2.1.1, and interpret the adjusted slope.

Solution

Alone: \(\tilde\beta = 1.191\) mpg per second (\(R^2 = 0.177\)). With weight: \(\hat\beta_{\text{acc}} = 0.251\) (se 0.086, \(t = 2.91\)), \(\hat\beta_{\text{w}} = -7.336\), \(R^2 = 0.698\). The slope of weight on acceleration is \(-0.1282\) thousand lb per second (slower cars are lighter), and \(0.251 + (-7.336)(-0.1282) = 0.251 + 0.940 = 1.191\). Most of the marginal association is weight: slow-accelerating cars are light, and light cars are economical. Among cars of the same weight, one extra second of acceleration time goes with only 0.25 mpg higher average fuel economy.

C.2 (★★). For the 392 cars with horsepower recorded, fit mpg on weight (1000 lb) and horsepower (100 hp), separately and together. Compare the slopes and \(R^2\) values and verify Theorem 2.1.1 for horsepower. What do the results suggest about separating the roles of weight and power?

Solution

Weight alone: \(-7.647\) (\(R^2 = 0.693\)). Horsepower alone: \(-15.784\) per 100 hp (\(R^2 = 0.606\)). Together: weight \(-5.794\) (se 0.502), horsepower \(-4.730\) (se 1.109), \(R^2 = 0.706\). The slope of weight on horsepower is \(1.9078\) thousand lb per 100 hp, and \(-4.730 + (-5.794)(1.9078) = -15.784\). Weight and horsepower are highly correlated (\(r = 0.865\)), so most of the marginal horsepower slope is indirect, the joint fit improves \(R^2\) only a little over weight alone, and the separate slopes have larger standard errors than in the weight + year model. Separating highly correlated predictors is the subject of Module 5.

C.3 (★). Plot weight against model year and mark the point (4500 lb, 1982). Then predict mpg at (2500 lb, 1982) and compare with the prediction at (4500 lb, 1982). Which prediction is better supported by the data, and why?

Solution

Predictions: 31.2 mpg at (2500 lb, 1982) and 17.9 mpg at (4500 lb, 1982). The first lies inside the cloud of 1982 cars (weights 1965–3035 lb); the second lies in a region with no data, even though 4500 lb and 1982 are each inside their own ranges. Only the first is supported by nearby observations; the second relies entirely on the assumption that the plane, with a common weight slope for all years, extends to heavy recent cars.
