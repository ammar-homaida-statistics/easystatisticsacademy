1 Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. derive the normal equations \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\) and prove that, under full rank, \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\) is the unique least-squares estimate;
  2. interpret least squares as the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf{y}\) onto the column space of \(\mathbf{X}\);
  3. state and prove the properties of the hat matrix \(\mathbf{H}\) and of the residual vector, including \(\mathrm{SST} = \mathrm{SSR} + \mathrm{SSE}\);
  4. recover the simple-regression formulas of Module 1 as the case \(p = 1\);
  5. explain what happens when the full-rank condition fails, and why software computes \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) with a QR decomposition rather than by inverting \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\).

2 Motivation

Lesson 2.1 fitted mpg on weight and model year and simply reported the estimates. Lesson 2.2 wrote the model as \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\). This lesson connects the two: it derives the estimator for any number of predictors, shows that it has a simple geometric meaning, and introduces the hat matrix, which reappears in the distribution theory of Lesson 2.4 and in the diagnostics of Module 3. It also returns to the 4500-lb car of 1982 from Lesson 2.1 and measures how far it lies from the data.

Assumptions used in this lesson

The model is \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\) (Lesson 2.2), with

  • A0 (Full rank). \(\mathbf{X}\) is fixed with \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p + 1\).
  • A1 (Correct mean function). \(E(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \mathbf{0}\).
  • A2–A3 (Constant variance, uncorrelated errors). \(\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_n\).
  • A4 (Normality). \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim N_n(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_n)\).

This lesson uses only A0. Least squares is a computation on the observed data; no assumption about the errors is needed to define or compute it. A1–A4 enter in Lesson 2.4. What each assumption means: Lesson 1.1, Assumptions.

4 Theory

4.1 The least-squares problem

Least squares chooses \(\mathbf{b}\) to minimise \[ S(\mathbf{b}) = (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{b})^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{y} - 2\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}, \] using \(\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\) (a scalar equals its transpose). With the derivative rules of Lesson 2.2, \[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial\mathbf{b}} = -2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} + 2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} \qquad \text{(normal equations)}. \]

Theorem 2.3.1 (Least-squares estimator) — uses A0

  1. The normal equations always have at least one solution, and every solution minimises \(S(\mathbf{b})\).
  2. The fitted vector \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) is the same for every solution.
  3. Under A0 the solution is unique: \[ \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}. \]
Derivation

Existence. \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\) lies in the column space of \(\mathbf{X}^\top\), which equals the column space of \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) (both have the same rank, and \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}) \subseteq \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}^\top)\)). So the system is consistent.

Minimisation (no calculus needed). Let \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) solve the normal equations. For any \(\mathbf{b}\), \(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{b} = (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) + \mathbf{X}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \mathbf{b})\). The cross term vanishes because \((\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \mathbf{b})^\top\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = 0\) by the normal equations. Hence \[ S(\mathbf{b}) = S(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) + \lVert\mathbf{X}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \mathbf{b})\rVert^2 \ \ge\ S(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}). \]

Uniqueness of \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\). If \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_1\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_2\) both solve the normal equations, both attain the minimum, so the identity above gives \(\lVert\mathbf{X}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_1 - \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_2)\rVert^2 = 0\), i.e. \(\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_1 = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_2\).

Uniqueness of \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\). Under A0, \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) is invertible (Lemma 2.2.1, Lesson 2.2), so the normal equations have the single solution \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\). Equivalently, \(\mathbf{X}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_1 - \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_2) = \mathbf{0}\) forces \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_1 = \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_2\) when the columns of \(\mathbf{X}\) are linearly independent.

The second-derivative matrix is \(2\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\), positive definite under A0, which is the calculus way of seeing that the stationary point is the unique minimum. The completing-the-square argument above is stronger: it gives the global minimum and the size of the penalty, \(\lVert\mathbf{X}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \mathbf{b})\rVert^2\), for any other \(\mathbf{b}\).

4.2 Geometry: least squares is a projection

Every possible fitted vector \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}\) lies in the column space \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\), a \((p+1)\)-dimensional subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Least squares picks the point of \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\) closest to \(\mathbf{y}\). The normal equations say \(\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}) = \mathbf{0}\): the residual vector is orthogonal to every column of \(\mathbf{X}\). So \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf{y}\) onto \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\), and \[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}, \qquad \mathbf{e} = (\mathbf{I}_n - \mathbf{H})\mathbf{y}. \] \(\mathbf{H}\) “puts the hat on \(\mathbf{y}\)”. Its diagonal element \(h_{ii} = \partial\hat y_i/\partial y_i\) measures how strongly observation \(i\) pulls the fit towards itself; it is called the leverage.

Theorem 2.3.2 (Properties of the hat matrix) — uses A0

  1. \(\mathbf{H}\) and \(\mathbf{I}_n - \mathbf{H}\) are symmetric and idempotent, and \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{I}_n - \mathbf{H}) = \mathbf{0}\).
  2. \(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{X}\) and \((\mathbf{I}_n - \mathbf{H})\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{0}\).
  3. \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H}) = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{H}) = p + 1\) and \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{I}_n - \mathbf{H}) = n - p - 1\).
  4. \(0 \le h_{ii} \le 1\) for every \(i\), and \(\sum_i h_{ii} = p + 1\), so the average leverage is \((p+1)/n\).
Derivation
  1. \(\mathbf{H}^\top = \mathbf{X}[(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}]^\top\mathbf{X}^\top = \mathbf{H}\) because the inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric; \(\mathbf{H}^2 = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top = \mathbf{H}\). Then \((\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H})^2 = \mathbf{I} - 2\mathbf{H} + \mathbf{H}^2 = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H}\) and \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H}) = \mathbf{H} - \mathbf{H}^2 = \mathbf{0}\).
  2. \(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{X}\).
  3. By the cyclic property of the trace, \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H}) = \operatorname{tr}\bigl((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\bigr) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{I}_{p+1}) = p + 1\); for a symmetric idempotent matrix rank equals trace (Lesson 2.2, Exercise B.3).
  4. By symmetry and idempotence, \(h_{ii} = (\mathbf{H}^2)_{ii} = \sum_j h_{ij}h_{ji} = \sum_j h_{ij}^2 = h_{ii}^2 + \sum_{j \ne i} h_{ij}^2 \ge h_{ii}^2\), so \(h_{ii}^2 \le h_{ii}\), i.e. \(0 \le h_{ii} \le 1\). The sum is the trace.

4.3 Residuals and the decomposition of the total sum of squares

Theorem 2.3.3 (Properties of the residuals) — uses A0

  1. \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}^\top\mathbf{e} = 0\).
  2. If the model contains an intercept (a column \(\mathbf{1}\) in \(\mathbf{X}\)), then \(\sum_i e_i = 0\), the mean of the fitted values is \(\bar y\), and \[ \underbrace{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2}_{\mathrm{SST}} = \underbrace{\sum_i (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2}_{\mathrm{SSR}} + \underbrace{\sum_i e_i^2}_{\mathrm{SSE}} . \]
  3. If \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\), then \(\mathbf{e} = (\mathbf{I}_n - \mathbf{H})\boldsymbol\varepsilon\), whatever the value of \(\boldsymbol\beta\).
Derivation
  1. \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H})\mathbf{y} = [(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H})\mathbf{X}]^\top\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}\) by Theorem 2.3.2(b); then \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}^\top\mathbf{e} = \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{e} = 0\).
  2. The row of \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}\) that belongs to the column \(\mathbf{1}\) reads \(\sum_i e_i = 0\); hence \(\sum_i\hat y_i = \sum_i y_i\). Write \(\mathbf{y} - \bar y\mathbf{1} = (\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \bar y\mathbf{1}) + \mathbf{e}\); the cross product is \((\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \bar y\mathbf{1})^\top\mathbf{e} = \hat{\mathbf{y}}^\top\mathbf{e} - \bar y\,\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{e} = 0\), so squared lengths add.
  3. \(\mathbf{e} = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H})(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon) = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H})\boldsymbol\varepsilon\) by Theorem 2.3.2(b).

Part (c) is the starting point of Lesson 2.4: combined with Theorem 2.2.2 of Lesson 2.2 it gives \(E(\mathrm{SSE}) = \sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H}) = (n - p - 1)\sigma^2\). Without an intercept, (b) fails in general: the residuals need not sum to zero, and \(R^2\) computed as \(1 - \mathrm{SSE}/\mathrm{SST}\) loses its usual meaning.

4.4 Simple regression as the case \(p = 1\)

With \(\mathbf{X} = [\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{x}]\), \[ \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix} n & \sum x_i\\ \sum x_i & \sum x_i^2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1} = \frac{1}{nS_{xx}}\begin{pmatrix} \sum x_i^2 & -\sum x_i\\ -\sum x_i & n \end{pmatrix}, \] since the determinant is \(n\sum x_i^2 - (\sum x_i)^2 = nS_{xx}\). Multiplying by \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} = (\sum y_i, \sum x_iy_i)^\top\) returns \(\hat\beta_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx}\) and \(\hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1\bar x\), the formulas of Lesson 1.3 (Exercise B.1). The full-rank condition A0 is \(S_{xx} > 0\): the \(x_i\) are not all equal.

4.5 When the full-rank condition fails

If a column of \(\mathbf{X}\) is a linear combination of the others, Theorem 2.3.1 still gives a unique fitted vector \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\), residuals and SSE, but infinitely many coefficient vectors produce them. The coefficients of the dependent columns are not identifiable from the data. Software reacts differently: R’s lm() detects the dependence during its QR decomposition and reports NA for the redundant coefficient; NumPy’s lstsq returns the solution with the smallest Euclidean norm. Neither is “the” estimate; the remedy is to remove or re-define the redundant column (Lesson 2.9 for indicator variables).

4.6 Computing \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) in practice

The formula \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\) is for derivations, not for computation. Forming \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) squares the condition number: \(\kappa(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}) = \kappa(\mathbf{X})^2\) (2-norm condition numbers), so about twice as many significant digits can be lost. Statistical software uses the QR decomposition \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}\), where \(\mathbf{Q}^\top\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}_{p+1}\) and \(\mathbf{R}\) is upper triangular and, under A0, invertible. Substituting into the normal equations gives \(\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{Q}^\top\mathbf{y}\), i.e. \[ \mathbf{R}\,\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf{Q}^\top\mathbf{y}, \qquad \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^\top, \qquad h_{ii} = \sum_k q_{ik}^2, \] a triangular system solved by back-substitution, without ever forming \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\).

5 Worked example

The running model is mpg on weight (1000 lb) and model year for all \(n = 398\) cars, so \(p = 2\).

5.1 Three ways to compute \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\)

Solving the normal equations, solving \(\mathbf{R}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf{Q}^\top\mathbf{y}\), and calling lm() all give \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (-14.198, -6.664, 0.757)^\top\), the values of Lesson 2.1. The first two differ by less than \(10^{-11}\) here. The condition numbers are \(\kappa(\mathbf{X}) \approx 1756\) and \(\kappa(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}) \approx 3.08\times10^6 = 1756^2\): harmless for this model, but Exercise C.3 shows a design where the normal equations fail.

R code 
cols <- c("mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
          "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name")
auto <- read.table("data/auto-mpg.data", col.names = cols, na.strings = "?")
auto$w <- auto$weight / 1000
X <- model.matrix(~ w + model_year, data = auto)
y <- auto$mpg

b_ne <- solve(crossprod(X), crossprod(X, y))          # normal equations
qx <- qr(X)
b_qr <- qr.coef(qx, y)                                 # QR: solves R b = Q'y
fit <- lm(mpg ~ w + model_year, data = auto)
cbind(normal_eq = drop(b_ne), qr = b_qr, lm = coef(fit))
max(abs(drop(b_ne) - b_qr))
c(kappa_X = kappa(X, exact = TRUE), kappa_XtX = kappa(crossprod(X), exact = TRUE))
Python code 
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

cols = ["mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
        "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name"]
auto = pd.read_csv("data/auto-mpg.data", sep=r"\s+", header=None,
                   names=cols, na_values="?", quotechar='"')
y = auto["mpg"].to_numpy()
w = auto["weight"].to_numpy() / 1000
yr = auto["model_year"].to_numpy().astype(float)
n = len(y)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), w, yr])

b_ne = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)              # normal equations
Q, R = np.linalg.qr(X)                                 # reduced QR: Q is n x 3
b_qr = np.linalg.solve(R, Q.T @ y)                     # R b = Q'y (triangular)
print("normal equations:", b_ne.round(6))
print("QR:              ", b_qr.round(6))
print("max difference:  ", f"{np.abs(b_ne - b_qr).max():.1e}")
print(f"cond(X) = {np.linalg.cond(X):.1f}, cond(X'X) = {np.linalg.cond(X.T @ X):.4g}")

5.2 The hat matrix and the residuals

The numerical results confirm Theorems 2.3.2 and 2.3.3: \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H}) = 3\); \(\sum e_i\) and every entry of \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{e}\) are zero up to rounding (below \(2\times10^{-9}\)); and \[ \mathrm{SST} = 24252.58 = \mathrm{SSR} + \mathrm{SSE} = 19592.74 + 4659.84, \qquad R^2 = 0.8079 . \] The average leverage is \(3/398 = 0.00754\). The leverages range from \(0.00252\) (a car close to the average weight and year; Exercise B.2 shows \(h_{ii} \ge 1/n = 0.00251\)) to \(0.0199\) for the Pontiac Safari station wagon, at 5140 lb the heaviest car in the data, from 1971.

5.3 Leverage and hidden extrapolation

The leverage idea extends to a new point \(\mathbf{x}_0\): \(h_{00} = \mathbf{x}_0^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_0\) measures how far \(\mathbf{x}_0\) lies from the centre of the data, taking the correlation between the predictors into account (for \(p = 1\) it is the \(h_0\) of Lesson 1.8). Lesson 2.1 found that a 4500-lb car from 1982 lies inside both separate ranges but in an empty part of the (weight, year) plane. Its leverage makes this precise:

New car \(h_{00}\) Compared with the data
3000 lb, 1976 0.0025 near the minimum leverage: the centre of the data
2500 lb, 1982 0.0091 typical of the observed cars
4500 lb, 1982 0.0239 larger than every observed car (maximum 0.0199)

A new point whose leverage exceeds the largest \(h_{ii}\) in the data is outside the region the data cover: a practical check for hidden extrapolation in any number of dimensions.

R code 
# Uses auto, X and h from Sections 5.1-5.2 (run that code first).
XtX_inv <- solve(crossprod(X))
new <- rbind(c(1, 3.0, 76), c(1, 2.5, 82), c(1, 4.5, 82))
h00 <- rowSums((new %*% XtX_inv) * new)
round(h00, 4)
plot(auto$weight, auto$model_year + 1900, cex = 0.4 + 60 * h, pch = 1,
     xlab = "Weight (lb)", ylab = "Model year", xlim = c(1500, 5200), ylim = c(1969.5, 1983.8))
points(new[, 2] * 1000, new[, 3] + 1900, pch = 4, cex = 2, lwd = 3, col = "red")
text(new[, 2] * 1000, new[, 3] + 1900, sprintf("h00 = %.4f", h00), pos = 3, col = "red")
Python code 
# Uses np, auto, X and h from Sections 5.1-5.2 (run that code first).
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

XtX_inv = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
new = np.array([[1, 3.0, 76], [1, 2.5, 82], [1, 4.5, 82]])
h00 = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", new, XtX_inv, new)
print("h00:", h00.round(4), " max h in data:", h.max().round(4))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 4.2))
ax.scatter(auto["weight"], auto["model_year"] + 1900, s=5 + 2500 * h,
           facecolors="none", edgecolors="gray", linewidths=0.8)
ax.scatter(new[:, 1] * 1000, new[:, 2] + 1900, marker="x", s=90, linewidths=3, color="red")
for (_, wt, yy), hh in zip(new, h00):
    ax.annotate(f"h00 = {hh:.4f}", (wt * 1000, yy + 1900), textcoords="offset points",
                xytext=(0, 8), ha="center", color="red")
ax.set_ylim(1969.5, 1983.8)
ax.set_xlabel("Weight (lb)"); ax.set_ylabel("Model year")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

5.4 When the full-rank condition fails: weight in pounds and kilograms

Adding weight in kilograms to a model that already contains weight in pounds makes the third column \(0.45359237\) times the second, so \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = 2 < 3\). The fitted values are identical (to \(10^{-13}\)) to those of the model with weight in pounds alone, as Theorem 2.3.1(b) predicts. R’s lm() reports NA for the kilogram coefficient. NumPy’s lstsq returns the minimum-norm solution, which splits the slope between the two columns: \(-0.006367\) per lb and \(-0.002888\) per kg. Converted to pounds, \(-0.006367 + 0.45359237 \times (-0.002888) = -0.007677\) mpg per lb, the slope of the one-predictor model; only this combination is identified.

R code 
# Uses auto and y from Section 5.1 (run that code first).
auto$weight_kg <- auto$weight * 0.45359237
fit_both <- lm(mpg ~ weight + weight_kg, data = auto)
coef(fit_both)                                         # NA for the aliased column
fit_lb <- lm(mpg ~ weight, data = auto)
max(abs(fitted(fit_both) - fitted(fit_lb)))

X_dep = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), auto["weight"], auto["weight"] * 0.45359237])

6 Common misconceptions

Misconceptions

  1. “Least squares needs normally distributed errors.” — Computing \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) needs only A0. The error assumptions matter for the properties of \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) (Lesson 2.4).
  2. “Software computes \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\).” — Good software uses QR (or similar) decompositions, because forming \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) squares the condition number.
  3. “If \(\mathbf{X}\) is rank deficient, the regression cannot be fitted.” — The fitted values and residuals are unique; only the coefficients of the dependent columns are not identified.
  4. “The residuals always sum to zero.” — Only when the model contains an intercept (or, more generally, when \(\mathbf{1}\) lies in \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\)).
  5. “High leverage means a bad observation.” — Leverage depends only on \(\mathbf{X}\). It says an observation can strongly influence the fit; whether it does depends on its response (Module 3).

7 Key takeaways

  • The normal equations \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\) always have a solution; under full rank it is unique, \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\).
  • Least squares projects \(\mathbf{y}\) orthogonally onto the column space of \(\mathbf{X}\): \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}\), and the residuals are orthogonal to every column of \(\mathbf{X}\).
  • \(\mathbf{H}\) is symmetric and idempotent with trace \(p + 1\); the leverages \(h_{ii}\) lie in \([0, 1]\) and average \((p+1)/n\).
  • With an intercept, the residuals sum to zero and \(\mathrm{SST} = \mathrm{SSR} + \mathrm{SSE}\).
  • \(\mathbf{e} = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H})\boldsymbol\varepsilon\): the residuals are a projection of the errors, not the errors themselves.
  • A new point with leverage above the largest observed \(h_{ii}\) signals hidden extrapolation.

8 Exercises

8.1 A. Conceptual

A.1 (★). Explain, using Theorem 2.3.3, why including an intercept guarantees that the residuals sum to zero. Give a reason why one might fit a model without an intercept, and say what is lost.

Solution

The normal equations state \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}\), one equation per column. If \(\mathbf{1}\) is a column, its equation is \(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{e} = \sum e_i = 0\). A no-intercept model is sometimes justified by theory (e.g. zero input must give zero output). Without the intercept, \(\sum e_i\) need not be zero, the decomposition \(\mathrm{SST} = \mathrm{SSR} + \mathrm{SSE}\) with \(\mathrm{SST}\) about \(\bar y\) fails, and the usual \(R^2\) is no longer comparable with models that include an intercept; software then reports an “uncentred” \(R^2\) instead.

A.2 (★). In the pounds-and-kilograms model of Section 5.4, which of the following are uniquely determined by the data: \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\), \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\), \(\mathbf{e}\), SSE, \(R^2\), \(\hat\beta_{\text{lb}} + 0.45359237\,\hat\beta_{\text{kg}}\)?

Solution

All except \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) itself. \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) is unique (Theorem 2.3.1b), hence so are \(\mathbf{e}\), SSE and \(R^2\). The combination \(\hat\beta_{\text{lb}} + 0.45359237\,\hat\beta_{\text{kg}}\) is the slope per pound of the fitted values, which is unique; the separate coefficients are not.

8.2 B. Mathematical

B.1 (★★). For \(p = 1\), use \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\) from Section 4.4 to derive \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\), and show that the leverage is \(h_{ii} = 1/n + (x_i - \bar x)^2/S_{xx}\).

Solution

\(\hat\beta_1 = \bigl(-\sum x_i\sum y_i + n\sum x_iy_i\bigr)/(nS_{xx}) = \bigl(\sum x_iy_i - n\bar x\bar y\bigr)/S_{xx} = S_{xy}/S_{xx}\), and \(\hat\beta_0 = \bigl(\sum x_i^2\sum y_i - \sum x_i\sum x_iy_i\bigr)/(nS_{xx})\); substituting \(\sum x_i^2 = S_{xx} + n\bar x^2\) and \(\sum x_iy_i = S_{xy} + n\bar x\bar y\) gives \(\hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1\bar x\). For the leverage, \(h_{ii} = (1, x_i)(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}(1, x_i)^\top = \bigl(\sum x_j^2 - 2x_i\sum x_j + nx_i^2\bigr)/(nS_{xx})\). The numerator is \(S_{xx} + n\bar x^2 - 2nx_i\bar x + nx_i^2 = S_{xx} + n(x_i - \bar x)^2\), so \(h_{ii} = 1/n + (x_i - \bar x)^2/S_{xx}\).

B.2 (★★). Suppose \(\mathbf{X}\) contains the column \(\mathbf{1}\). Show that \(h_{ii} \ge 1/n\) for every \(i\). Hint: show that \(\mathbf{H} - \tfrac1n\mathbf{J}_n\) is symmetric and idempotent.

Solution

Since \(\mathbf{1} \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\), \(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}\) (Theorem 2.3.2b), so \(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top = \mathbf{J}\) and, by symmetry, \(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}\). With \(\mathbf{J}^2 = n\mathbf{J}\), \((\mathbf{H} - \tfrac1n\mathbf{J})^2 = \mathbf{H} - \tfrac2n\mathbf{J} + \tfrac1{n^2}n\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{H} - \tfrac1n\mathbf{J}\). A symmetric idempotent matrix has non-negative diagonal elements (same argument as Theorem 2.3.2d), so \(h_{ii} - 1/n \ge 0\). In the worked example, the smallest leverage, \(0.00252\), is just above \(1/398 = 0.00251\).

B.3 (★★). Let \(\tilde{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{A}\) with \(\mathbf{A}\) a non-singular \((p+1)\times(p+1)\) matrix. Show that \(\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \mathbf{H}\), so fitted values and residuals do not change, and that \(\tilde{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\). Use this to explain the centred intercept of Lesson 2.1 (weight at 3000 lb, year at 1976): \(23.310 = -14.198 + 3(-6.664) + 76(0.757)\).

Solution

\(\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}(\mathbf{A}^\top)^{-1}\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top = \mathbf{H}\). (Geometrically: \(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{A}) = \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{X})\).) Then \(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) with \(\tilde{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{A}\) gives \(\tilde{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) by uniqueness. Centring: the columns \((1, w - 3, \text{year} - 76)\) equal \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{A}\) with \(\mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & -3 & -76\\ 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\), whose inverse has first row \((1, 3, 76)\). So the new intercept is \(\hat\beta_0 + 3\hat\beta_1 + 76\hat\beta_2 = -14.198 - 19.992 + 57.500 = 23.310\), and the slopes are unchanged.

8.3 C. Computational

C.1 (★). For the 392 cars with horsepower recorded, fit mpg on weight (1000 lb) and horsepower (100 hp) by QR. Verify \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}\) and \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H}) = 3\), find the car with the largest leverage, and compute \(h_{00}\) for a hypothetical 2000-lb car with 200 hp. Is a prediction for that car supported by the data?

Solution

\(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (45.640, -5.794, -4.730)\); \(\max|\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{e}| \approx 3\times10^{-11}\); \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H}) = 3\). C.3 (★★★). Normal equations versus QR. Fit mpg on a polynomial in model year, using the raw powers \(1, t, t^2, \dots, t^d\) of the recorded year \(t\) (70–82), for \(d = 4\) and \(d = 5\). Compute the coefficients (i) by solving the normal equations and (ii) by QR, and compare the SSE values. Which answer is wrong, and how can you tell without knowing the truth? (For a reference value, refit with the rescaled variable \(s = (t - 76)/6\).)

Solution (Python) 
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
cols = ["mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
        "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name"]
auto = pd.read_csv("data/auto-mpg.data", sep=r"\s+", header=None,
                   names=cols, na_values="?", quotechar='"')
y = auto["mpg"].to_numpy(); t = auto["model_year"].to_numpy().astype(float)
for d in (4, 5):
    P = np.column_stack([t**k for k in range(d + 1)])
    b_ne = np.linalg.solve(P.T @ P, P.T @ y)
    Q, R = np.linalg.qr(P); b_qr = np.linalg.solve(R, Q.T @ y)
    S = np.column_stack([((t - 76) / 6)**k for k in range(d + 1)])
    b_s = np.linalg.lstsq(S, y, rcond=None)[0]
    sse = lambda M, b: np.sum((y - M @ b)**2)
    print(d, f"cond = {np.linalg.cond(P):.2g}", round(sse(P, b_ne), 3),
          round(sse(P, b_qr), 3), round(sse(S, b_s), 3))

Output: for \(d = 4\) (condition number \(9.3\times10^{12}\)) all three SSE values agree, 15170.405. For \(d = 5\) (condition number \(1.8\times10^{16}\)) the normal equations give SSE \(= 15949.675\) on our computer (the exact wrong value depends on the numerical library), while QR and the rescaled fit give \(15148.450\). The normal-equation answer is wrong, and this can be seen without a reference: the degree-5 model contains the degree-4 model, so its minimum SSE cannot exceed 15170.405. Squaring the condition number pushed it beyond double-precision accuracy (\(\approx 10^{16}\)). Rescaling the predictor (here to \(s\)) is the practical cure; orthogonal polynomials go further (Lesson 2.11). In R, try the same with solve(crossprod(P), crossprod(P, y)) and with qr.coef(qr(P), y) and compare what happens.
