1 Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. construct and interpret \(t\)-tests and confidence intervals for a single coefficient \(\beta_j\) and for any linear combination \(\mathbf{a}^\top\boldsymbol\beta\);
  2. compute a confidence interval for the mean response and a prediction interval for a new observation at \(\mathbf{x}_0\), and explain why the second is wider;
  3. derive the \(F\)-based joint confidence region for several linear combinations and draw it for two coefficients;
  4. explain why separate 95% intervals do not give 95% joint coverage, and use Bonferroni and Scheffé intervals;
  5. recognise when a combination of parameter values is ruled out jointly although each value is plausible on its own.

2 Motivation

Lesson 2.5 found that, given weight and model year, horsepower has a slope of \(-0.50\) with a standard error of \(0.94\). Is that slope distinguishable from zero? What fuel economy should we expect from a particular car, and how far might one car be from that expectation? And when we report intervals for several coefficients at once, what is the chance that all of them are right? These questions use the distribution theory of Lesson 2.4; this lesson turns it into procedures. They generalise Lesson 1.7 and Lesson 1.8 to any number of predictors.

3 Notation for this lesson

Symbol Meaning
\(m = n - p - 1\) residual degrees of freedom
\(t_{m;\,q}\), \(F_{q_1,q_2;\,q}\) \(q\)-quantiles of the \(t_m\) and \(F_{q_1,q_2}\) distributions
\(c_{jj}\) \(j\)-th diagonal element of \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\); \(\operatorname{se}(\hat\beta_j) = \hat\sigma\sqrt{c_{jj}}\)
\(\mathbf{x}_0\), \(h_{00} = \mathbf{x}_0^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_0\) a new predictor vector (with leading 1) and its leverage
\(\mu_0 = \mathbf{x}_0^\top\boldsymbol\beta\), \(y_0\) mean response at \(\mathbf{x}_0\), and a new observation there
\(\mathbf{L}\) (\(q\times(p+1)\), rank \(q\)) matrix of \(q\) linear combinations \(\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol\beta\)

Assumptions used in this lesson

The model is \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\) (Lesson 2.2), with

  • A0 (Full rank). \(\mathbf{X}\) is fixed with \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p + 1\).
  • A1 (Correct mean function). \(E(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \mathbf{0}\).
  • A2–A3 (Constant variance, uncorrelated errors). \(\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_n\).
  • A4 (Normality). \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim N_n(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_n)\).

All exact results in this lesson use A0–A4. For large \(n\), the intervals for coefficients and mean responses remain approximately valid without A4 (Lesson 2.4, Section 5.3); prediction intervals do not, because they depend on the distribution of a single new error. What each assumption means: Lesson 1.1, Assumptions.

4 Theory

4.1 One coefficient or one linear combination

Theorem 2.6.1 (\(t\)-procedures) — uses A0–A4

For a fixed \(\mathbf{a} \ne \mathbf{0}\), a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mathbf{a}^\top\boldsymbol\beta\) is \[ \mathbf{a}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} \pm t_{m;\,1-\alpha/2}\;\hat\sigma\sqrt{\mathbf{a}^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{a}}, \] and the test of \(H_0\colon \mathbf{a}^\top\boldsymbol\beta = c\) rejects at level \(\alpha\) when \(|T| > t_{m;\,1-\alpha/2}\), with \(T = (\mathbf{a}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - c)/\bigl(\hat\sigma\sqrt{\mathbf{a}^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{a}}\bigr)\). With \(\mathbf{a}\) the \(j\)-th unit vector: \(\hat\beta_j \pm t_{m;\,1-\alpha/2}\operatorname{se}(\hat\beta_j)\).

The proof is one line: under \(H_0\), \(T \sim t_m\) by Theorem 2.4.5(d), Lesson 2.4, and the interval is the set of \(c\) not rejected.

What does \(H_0\colon\beta_j = 0\) test? By the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem of Lesson 2.5, it asks whether \(x_j\) is linearly related to \(y\) after removing the other predictors from both: whether \(x_j\) adds anything to a model that already contains them. A non-significant \(\beta_j\) does not mean \(x_j\) is unrelated to \(y\), and its answer changes when the set of other predictors changes.

4.2 Mean response and prediction

Theorem 2.6.2 (Intervals at a new point) — uses A0–A4

Let \(\hat y_0 = \mathbf{x}_0^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\). (a) A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for the mean response \(\mu_0 = \mathbf{x}_0^\top\boldsymbol\beta\) is \(\hat y_0 \pm t_{m;\,1-\alpha/2}\,\hat\sigma\sqrt{h_{00}}\). (b) If a new observation \(y_0 = \mu_0 + \varepsilon_0\) has \(\varepsilon_0 \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\) independent of \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\), a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) prediction interval for \(y_0\) is \(\hat y_0 \pm t_{m;\,1-\alpha/2}\,\hat\sigma\sqrt{1 + h_{00}}\).

Derivation
  1. is Theorem 2.6.1 with \(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{x}_0\). (b) The prediction error \(y_0 - \hat y_0 = \varepsilon_0 - \mathbf{x}_0^\top(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \boldsymbol\beta)\) is normal with mean 0 and variance \(\sigma^2 + \sigma^2h_{00}\), the two parts being independent. It depends on \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\) only through \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\), and \(\varepsilon_0\) is independent of \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\), so it is independent of SSE (Theorem 2.4.5c). Hence \((y_0 - \hat y_0)/(\hat\sigma\sqrt{1 + h_{00}}) \sim t_m\).

The two intervals answer different questions. The confidence interval is for the average fuel economy of all cars with predictor values \(\mathbf{x}_0\); its width shrinks to zero as \(n \to \infty\). The prediction interval is for one car; its width never falls below about \(2t\sigma\), because a single car varies around the average by \(\sigma\). Both widen with the leverage \(h_{00}\): far from the data, even the average is poorly known. For \(p = 1\), \(h_{00} = 1/n + (x_0 - \bar x)^2/S_{xx}\) and Theorem 2.6.2 combines Theorems 1.8.1 and 1.8.2 of Lesson 1.8.

4.3 Several linear combinations at once

Theorem 2.6.3 (Joint confidence region) — uses A0–A4

Let \(\mathbf{L}\) be a fixed \(q\times(p+1)\) matrix of rank \(q\) and \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{L}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{L}^\top\). Then \[ F = \frac{(\mathbf{L}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \mathbf{L}\boldsymbol\beta)^\top\mathbf{W}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \mathbf{L}\boldsymbol\beta)}{q\,\hat\sigma^2} \sim F_{q,\,m}, \] and \(\{\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathbb{R}^q : (\mathbf{L}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \boldsymbol\theta)^\top\mathbf{W}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \boldsymbol\theta) \le q\,\hat\sigma^2F_{q,m;\,1-\alpha}\}\) is a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence region for \(\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol\beta\): an ellipsoid centred at \(\mathbf{L}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\).

Derivation

\(\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{L}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \mathbf{L}\boldsymbol\beta \sim N_q(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2\mathbf{W})\), and \(\mathbf{W}\) is positive definite because \(\mathbf{L}\) has full row rank. Let \(\mathbf{W}^{-1/2}\) be the symmetric inverse square root. Then \(\mathbf{W}^{-1/2}\mathbf{Z}/\sigma \sim N_q(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}_q)\), so \(\mathbf{Z}^\top\mathbf{W}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}/\sigma^2\), the sum of squares of its entries, is \(\chi^2_q\). It is a function of \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\), hence independent of \(\mathrm{SSE}/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_m\) (Theorem 2.4.5b–c). The ratio of the two, each divided by its degrees of freedom, is \(F_{q,m}\); the \(\sigma^2\) cancels and leaves the statistic above. The region is the set of \(\boldsymbol\theta\) for which \(F \le F_{q,m;\,1-\alpha}\).

For \(q = 1\), \(F = T^2\) and the region is the \(t\)-interval. For \(q = 2\) the region is an ellipse whose shape is set by \(\mathbf{W}\): if the two estimates are strongly correlated, the ellipse is long and thin and tilted, and the rectangle formed by the two separate intervals describes it badly. Lesson 2.7 uses the same statistic, with \(\boldsymbol\theta\) fixed by a hypothesis, as the general \(F\)-test.

4.4 Simultaneous intervals: Bonferroni and Scheffé

If each of \(q\) separate intervals has coverage \(1 - \alpha\), the probability that all of them cover is generally less than \(1 - \alpha\). Two standard remedies keep the convenient interval form:

  • Bonferroni: use \(t_{m;\,1-\alpha/(2q)}\) in place of \(t_{m;\,1-\alpha/2}\) for each of the \(q\) intervals. By Boole’s inequality, \(P(\text{some interval misses}) \le q\cdot\alpha/q = \alpha\), so the joint coverage is at least \(1 - \alpha\), whatever the correlations (Exercise B.3).
  • Scheffé: use the multiplier \(\sqrt{q\,F_{q,m;\,1-\alpha}}\). The resulting intervals for all linear combinations \(\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol\beta\) hold simultaneously with probability exactly \(1 - \alpha\), because they are the projections of the ellipsoid of Theorem 2.6.3. The Working–Hotelling band of Lesson 1.8 is the case \(p = 1\), \(q = 2\).

Bonferroni is better for a few pre-specified intervals; Scheffé is better when many combinations, possibly chosen after looking at the data, are of interest.

5 Worked example

We continue with the 392 cars and the model mpg ~ weight (1000 lb) + model year + horsepower (100 hp) of Lesson 2.5: \(m = 388\), \(\hat\sigma = 3.430\), \(t_{388;\,0.975} = 1.966\).

Python code 
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

cols = ["mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
        "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name"]
auto = pd.read_csv("data/auto-mpg.data", sep=r"\s+", header=None,
                   names=cols, na_values="?", quotechar='"').dropna().reset_index(drop=True)
y = auto["mpg"].to_numpy()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), auto["weight"] / 1000, auto["model_year"],
                     auto["horsepower"] / 100])
n, k = X.shape; m = n - k
XtX_inv = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
b = XtX_inv @ X.T @ y
e = y - X @ b
s = np.sqrt(e @ e / m)
se = s * np.sqrt(np.diag(XtX_inv))
q = stats.t.ppf(0.975, m)
t = b / se
print(f"df = {m}, sigma_hat = {s:.4f}, t_crit = {q:.4f}")
print(pd.DataFrame({"estimate": b, "se": se, "t": t, "p": 2 * stats.t.sf(np.abs(t), m),
                    "lower": b - q * se, "upper": b + q * se},
                   index=["intercept", "weight", "year", "horsepower"]).round(4).to_string())

5.3 A joint confidence region for two slopes

The estimates of the weight and horsepower slopes have correlation \(-0.851\) (Lesson 2.4: strongly correlated predictors give strongly correlated estimates). With \(q = 2\), \(F_{2,388;\,0.95} = 3.019\) and Theorem 2.6.3 gives a long, thin, tilted ellipse.

  • The point \((\beta_{\text{weight}}, \beta_{\text{hp}}) = (-5.7, 1.3)\) lies inside both separate 95% intervals, yet its \(F\)-value is \(23.4\): it is far outside the joint region. Each value is plausible alone; the combination is not, because the data say that a less negative weight slope must go with a more negative horsepower slope.
  • The point \((-7.4, 1.6)\) lies outside both separate intervals, but its \(F\)-value is \(2.82 < 3.019\): it is inside the joint region.

The Bonferroni intervals for the two slopes (\(t_{388;\,0.9875} = 2.250\)) are \((-7.368, -5.528)\) for weight and \((-2.624, 1.624)\) for horsepower: slightly wider than the separate intervals, and still a rectangle.

R code 
# Uses auto and fit from Section 5.1 (run that code first).
b <- coef(fit); V <- vcov(fit); m <- df.residual(fit)
idx <- c("w", "hp"); bS <- b[idx]; VS <- V[idx, idx]
cov2cor(VS)
Fq <- qf(0.95, 2, m)
Fstat <- function(theta) drop(t(bS - theta) %*% solve(VS) %*% (bS - theta)) / 2
c(F_A = Fstat(c(-5.7, 1.3)), F_B = Fstat(c(-7.4, 1.6)), F_crit = Fq)

ang <- seq(0, 2 * pi, length.out = 400)
ell <- t(bS + t(chol(VS)) %*% rbind(cos(ang), sin(ang)) * sqrt(2 * Fq))
ci <- confint(fit)[idx, ]
bonf <- cbind(bS - qt(1 - 0.05 / 4, m) * sqrt(diag(VS)), bS + qt(1 - 0.05 / 4, m) * sqrt(diag(VS)))

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.patches import Rectangle

idx = [1, 3]                                            # weight, horsepower
bS = b[idx]; VS = s**2 * XtX_inv[np.ix_(idx, idx)]
print("correlation of the two estimates:", round(VS[0, 1] / np.sqrt(VS[0, 0] * VS[1, 1]), 4))
Fq = stats.f.ppf(0.95, 2, m)
Fstat = lambda th: (bS - th) @ np.linalg.solve(VS, bS - th) / 2

ang = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 400)
ell = bS[:, None] + np.linalg.cholesky(VS) @ np.vstack([np.cos(ang), np.sin(ang)]) * np.sqrt(2 * Fq)

print("Bonferroni intervals:", np.c_[bS - qb * se[idx], bS + qb * se[idx]].round(4))

ax.plot(ell[0], ell[1], linewidth=2, color="black")
for mult, style in ((q, "--"), (qb, ":")):
    lo = bS - mult * se[idx]

ax.plot(*bS, "ko")
for (x0, y0), lab in (((-5.7, 1.3), "A"), ((-7.4, 1.6), "B")):
    ax.plot(x0, y0, "x", color="red", markersize=9, markeredgewidth=2)
    ax.annotate(lab, (x0, y0), textcoords="offset points", xytext=(0, 7), ha="center", color="red")

5.4 Simulation: individual, Bonferroni and joint coverage

Keep the 392 predictor rows, set \(\boldsymbol\beta = (-13.7, -6.45, 0.749, -0.50)^\top\) and \(\sigma = 3.43\), and simulate 10,000 data sets. Results (Python, seed 2026):

Procedure for (weight, horsepower) Covers both slopes
Two separate 95% \(t\)-intervals 0.927
Two Bonferroni intervals (\(\alpha/2\) each) 0.962
95% joint ellipse (Theorem 2.6.3) 0.949

Each separate interval covers its own slope about 95% of the time (0.952 and 0.950), but both together only 92.7%. Bonferroni guarantees at least 95% and here gives 96.2%: conservative. The ellipse has the exact coverage. With \(q\) independent intervals the joint coverage would be \(0.95^q\); correlation between the estimates changes it, here upwards from \(0.9025\).

R code 
# Self-contained: reads the 392 predictor rows and needs nothing from earlier sections.
cols <- c("mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
          "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name")
auto <- na.omit(read.table("data/auto-mpg.data", col.names = cols, na.strings = "?"))
X <- cbind(1, auto$weight / 1000, auto$model_year, auto$horsepower / 100)
n <- nrow(X); m <- n - ncol(X)
beta <- c(-13.7, -6.45, 0.749, -0.50); sigma <- 3.43; R <- 10000
XtX_inv <- solve(crossprod(X)); A <- XtX_inv %*% t(X)
set.seed(2026)
Y <- matrix(drop(X %*% beta), R, n, byrow = TRUE) + matrix(rnorm(R * n, 0, sigma), R, n)
B <- Y %*% t(A); s2 <- rowSums((Y - B %*% t(X))^2) / m
idx <- c(2, 4); W_inv <- solve(XtX_inv[idx, idx])
D <- sweep(B[, idx], 2, beta[idx])                     # estimation errors
se1 <- sqrt(s2 * XtX_inv[2, 2]); se2 <- sqrt(s2 * XtX_inv[4, 4])
qi <- qt(0.975, m); qb <- qt(1 - 0.05 / 4, m); Fq <- qf(0.95, 2, m)
c1 <- abs(D[, 1]) <= qi * se1; c2 <- abs(D[, 2]) <= qi * se2
Fs <- rowSums((D %*% W_inv) * D) / (2 * s2)
c(weight_alone = mean(c1), hp_alone = mean(c2), both_separate = mean(c1 & c2),
  both_bonferroni = mean(abs(D[, 1]) <= qb * se1 & abs(D[, 2]) <= qb * se2),
  ellipse = mean(Fs <= Fq))
Python code 
# Self-contained: reads the 392 predictor rows and needs nothing from earlier sections.
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

cols = ["mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
        "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name"]
auto = pd.read_csv("data/auto-mpg.data", sep=r"\s+", header=None,
                   names=cols, na_values="?", quotechar='"').dropna()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(auto)), auto["weight"] / 1000, auto["model_year"],
                     auto["horsepower"] / 100])
n, k = X.shape; m = n - k
beta, sigma, R = np.array([-13.7, -6.45, 0.749, -0.50]), 3.43, 10_000
XtX_inv = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X); A = XtX_inv @ X.T
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
Y = X @ beta + sigma * rng.standard_normal((R, n))
B = Y @ A.T; s2 = ((Y - B @ X.T)**2).sum(axis=1) / m
idx = [1, 3]; W_inv = np.linalg.inv(XtX_inv[np.ix_(idx, idx)])
D = B[:, idx] - beta[idx]                                # estimation errors
se1, se2 = np.sqrt(s2 * XtX_inv[1, 1]), np.sqrt(s2 * XtX_inv[3, 3])
qi, qb, Fq = stats.t.ppf(0.975, m), stats.t.ppf(1 - 0.05 / 4, m), stats.f.ppf(0.95, 2, m)
c1, c2 = np.abs(D[:, 0]) <= qi * se1, np.abs(D[:, 1]) <= qi * se2
Fs = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", D, W_inv, D) / (2 * s2)
print({"weight_alone": c1.mean(), "hp_alone": c2.mean(), "both_separate": (c1 & c2).mean(),
       "both_bonferroni": ((np.abs(D[:, 0]) <= qb * se1) & (np.abs(D[:, 1]) <= qb * se2)).mean(),
       "ellipse": (Fs <= Fq).mean()})

Output 

{'weight_alone': np.float64(0.9523), 'hp_alone': np.float64(0.9504), 'both_separate': np.float64(0.9269), 'both_bonferroni': np.float64(0.9619), 'ellipse': np.float64(0.9492)}

6 Common misconceptions

Misconceptions

  1. “\(p = 0.60\) for horsepower shows it does not affect fuel economy.” — It shows that, given weight and model year, the data cannot distinguish its slope from zero; the interval \((-2.4, 1.4)\) also contains sizeable effects.
  2. “If each of two values lies in its 95% interval, the pair is plausible.” — Not when the estimates are correlated: point A is ruled out jointly (Section 5.3).
  3. “A 95% prediction interval contains 95% of future observations at every \(\mathbf{x}_0\).” — Only under the model, including constant variance and normal errors; for Auto MPG, Lesson 1.8 found coverage that varies with weight.
  4. “Ten 95% intervals are jointly 95%.” — Their joint coverage can be as low as 50%; use Bonferroni or Scheffé when joint statements are made.
  5. “A narrow confidence interval for the mean means accurate predictions.” — The mean can be known precisely while individual cars still vary by \(\pm2\sigma\).

7 Key takeaways

  • \(\mathbf{a}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} \pm t_{m;\,1-\alpha/2}\hat\sigma\sqrt{\mathbf{a}^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{a}}\) covers any single linear combination, including a coefficient ($ = $ unit vector) and a mean response (\(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{x}_0\)).
  • Prediction intervals add \(1\) under the square root: \(\hat\sigma\sqrt{1 + h_{00}}\).
  • A test of \(\beta_j = 0\) asks whether \(x_j\) adds to the other predictors, not whether it matters on its own.
  • Joint statements about \(q\) combinations use the \(F_{q,m}\) ellipsoid; separate intervals have lower joint coverage, and their rectangle misdescribes correlated estimates.
  • Bonferroni (\(\alpha/q\) per interval) is simple and conservative; Scheffé covers all combinations at once.

8 Exercises

8.1 A. Conceptual

A.1 (★). The horsepower slope has \(p = 0.597\) in the three-predictor model but \(p < 10^{-100}\) in a simple regression of mpg on horsepower. Explain both results in one or two sentences each, using Lesson 2.5.

Solution

Alone, horsepower stands in for weight and age as well, which are strongly related to fuel economy; its slope (\(-15.8\) per 100 hp) captures all of that. Given weight and model year, the remaining (partial) association is small and is estimated from the little independent variation horsepower has left (\(R_j^2 = 0.77\), VIF 4.4), so it is both small and imprecise.

A.2 (★★). Explain in words why point A \((-5.7, 1.3)\) in Section 5.3 is ruled out jointly although each coordinate is inside its own 95% interval. What feature of the data causes this?

Solution

The two estimates are strongly negatively correlated (\(-0.85\)), because weight and horsepower are strongly positively correlated: the data pin down a combination of the two slopes much better than each slope separately. Point A takes the weight slope near its upper limit and the horsepower slope near its upper limit; the data allow one of these only if the other moves the opposite way. Point A is therefore far from the ellipse along its short axis.

8.2 B. Mathematical

B.1 (★★). Show that the prediction interval of Theorem 2.6.2(b) is at least \(\sqrt{1 + 1/n}\) times as wide as an interval that knew \(\boldsymbol\beta\) (and used \(\hat\sigma\)), and that \(h_{00} \ge 1/n\) when the model has an intercept. Hint: write \(\mathbf{x}_0\) in centred form.

Solution

With known \(\boldsymbol\beta\) the interval would be \(\mu_0 \pm t\hat\sigma\); the prediction interval has \(\hat\sigma\sqrt{1 + h_{00}}\), so the ratio of widths is \(\sqrt{1 + h_{00}}\). With an intercept, reparametrise with centred predictors (Lesson 2.3, Exercise B.3; this does not change \(h_{00}\)). Then \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) is block diagonal with first entry \(n\), so \(h_{00} = 1/n + (\mathbf{x}_0^* - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^\top\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_0^* - \bar{\mathbf{x}}) \ge 1/n\), where \(\mathbf{x}_0^*\) holds the predictor values, \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}\) their means and \(\mathbf{S}\) the centred cross-product matrix (positive definite).

B.2 (★★). Show that for \(q = 1\) the region of Theorem 2.6.3 reduces to the interval of Theorem 2.6.1. Hint: \(F_{1,m;\,1-\alpha} = t_{m;\,1-\alpha/2}^2\).

Solution

With \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{a}^\top\), \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{a}^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{a}\) is a scalar and the region is \((\mathbf{a}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \theta)^2 \le \hat\sigma^2\mathbf{W}F_{1,m;\,1-\alpha}\). Since an \(F_{1,m}\) variable is the square of a \(t_m\) variable and the \(t\) distribution is symmetric, \(F_{1,m;\,1-\alpha} = t_{m;\,1-\alpha/2}^2\). Taking square roots gives \(|\mathbf{a}^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} - \theta| \le t_{m;\,1-\alpha/2}\hat\sigma\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}\).

B.3 (★★). Prove that \(q\) Bonferroni intervals, each with coverage \(1 - \alpha/q\), cover simultaneously with probability at least \(1 - \alpha\). When is the bound nearly attained?

Solution

Let \(A_k\) be the event that interval \(k\) misses. Then \(P(\text{all cover}) = 1 - P(\cup_k A_k) \ge 1 - \sum_k P(A_k) = 1 - q\cdot\alpha/q = 1 - \alpha\) (Boole’s inequality). The bound is nearly attained when the miss events rarely overlap, which happens for independent estimates with small \(\alpha\) (then \(P(\cup A_k) = 1 - (1 - \alpha/q)^q \approx \alpha\)); with strongly correlated estimates the misses overlap and Bonferroni is more conservative.

8.3 C. Computational

C.1 (★). For a car of 2200 lb from 1980 with 70 hp, report the 95% confidence interval for the mean and the 95% prediction interval, and explain the difference in width.

Solution

\(\hat y_0 = 31.64\), \(h_{00} = 0.0066\). Mean: \((31.10, 32.19)\); prediction: \((24.88, 38.41)\). The prediction interval is about \(\sqrt{1 + h_{00}}/\sqrt{h_{00}} \approx 12\) times wider, because it adds the variation of one car around the mean (\(\hat\sigma = 3.43\)) to the uncertainty of the estimated mean.

C.2 (★). Compute Bonferroni 95% intervals for all three slopes (\(q = 3\)) and compare them with the separate intervals.

Solution

\(t_{388;\,1 - 0.05/6} = 2.404\) (against \(1.966\)). Weight \((-7.431, -5.465)\); model year \((0.623, 0.874)\); horsepower \((-2.770, 1.770)\). Each is about 22% wider (\(2.404/1.966 = 1.22\)); in exchange the three statements hold jointly with probability at least 95%.

C.3 (★★). Test \(H_0\colon(\beta_{\text{weight}}, \beta_{\text{hp}}) = (-6.0, 1.2)\) with Theorem 2.6.3. Then check whether each value lies in its separate 95% interval. Reconcile the two answers.

Solution

\(F = 14.14\) on \((2, 388)\) df, \(p = 1.2\times10^{-6}\): rejected. Yet \(-6.0 \in (-7.252, -5.644)\) and \(1.2 \in (-2.356, 1.356)\). As for point A, the pair combines a weight slope above its estimate with a horsepower slope above its estimate, the direction the negatively correlated estimates make implausible. Separate intervals answer separate questions; the joint test answers the joint one.
