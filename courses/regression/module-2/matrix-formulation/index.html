1 Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. write the multiple regression model as \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\) and build the design matrix for a given model;
  2. state the model assumptions in matrix form, including the full-rank condition;
  3. use the matrix tools needed in Module 2: rank, trace, symmetric, idempotent and positive definite matrices, and derivatives of linear and quadratic forms;
  4. compute means and covariance matrices of linear transformations of random vectors, and the expectation of a quadratic form;
  5. state the definition and the linear-transformation property of the multivariate normal distribution.

2 Motivation

With \(p\) predictors, the scalar formulas of Module 1 become unmanageable: Exercise B.2 of Lesson 2.1 needed a page of algebra for two predictors. In matrix notation the model, the estimator and its properties take the same form for any \(p\), and every result of Module 1 reappears as a special case. This lesson collects the notation and the tools; Lesson 2.3 uses them to derive the least-squares estimator, and Lesson 2.4 to derive its distribution.

3 Notation for this lesson

Symbol Meaning
\(\mathbf{y}\) (\(n\times1\)) response vector
\(\mathbf{X}\) (\(n\times(p+1)\)) design matrix; first column \(\mathbf{1}\)
\(\boldsymbol\beta\) (\((p+1)\times1\)), \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\) (\(n\times1\)) coefficient vector, error vector

\(N_n(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\) \(n\)-variate normal distribution

Bold lower-case letters are column vectors, bold capitals are matrices, and \(^\top\) denotes the transpose (course notation standard).

4 Theory

4.1 The model in matrix form

Stacking the \(n\) equations \(y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_{i1} + \dots + \beta_px_{ip} + \varepsilon_i\) gives \[ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} y_1\\ y_2\\ \vdots\\ y_n \end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{y}} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_{11} & \cdots & x_{1p}\\ 1 & x_{21} & \cdots & x_{2p}\\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots\\ 1 & x_{n1} & \cdots & x_{np} \end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{X}} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \beta_0\\ \beta_1\\ \vdots\\ \beta_p \end{pmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\beta} + \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \varepsilon_1\\ \varepsilon_2\\ \vdots\\ \varepsilon_n \end{pmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon . \] Each column of \(\mathbf{X}\) is one regressor (the constant, then \(x_1,\dots,x_p\)); each row is one unit. Simple regression is the case \(p = 1\), with \(\mathbf{X} = [\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{x}]\).

Assumptions in matrix form

  • A0 (full rank). \(\mathbf{X}\) is fixed (non-random) with \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p + 1\); this requires \(n \ge p + 1\).
  • A1 (correct mean). \(E(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \mathbf{0}\), i.e. \(E(\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\).
  • A2–A3 (constant variance, uncorrelated errors). \(\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_n\).
  • A4 (normality, for exact inference). \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim N_n(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_n)\), equivalently \(\mathbf{y} \sim N_n(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta, \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_n)\).

A2–A3 are one statement: the diagonal entries of \(\sigma^2\mathbf{I}_n\) say \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2\), the off-diagonal zeros say \(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_i, \varepsilon_k) = 0\). A0 is new: it fails when one column of \(\mathbf{X}\) is a linear combination of the others (Lesson 2.1, Exercise B.2), and then the coefficients cannot be separated.

4.2 Matrix toolkit

The following facts are used throughout Module 2. Proofs that are standard linear algebra are omitted; see Seber and Lee (2003, Appendix A) or Searle (1982).

Transpose and inverse. \((\mathbf{AB})^\top = \mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{A}^\top\); \((\mathbf{AB})^{-1} = \mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\) for square invertible matrices; \((\mathbf{A}^{-1})^\top = (\mathbf{A}^\top)^{-1}\), so the inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric.

Rank. \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\) is the number of linearly independent columns (equivalently rows). \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\).

Trace. \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}) = \sum_i a_{ii}\) for square \(\mathbf{A}\). It is linear, and cyclic: \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{AB}) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{BA})\) whenever both products are defined. It equals the sum of the eigenvalues. A scalar equals its own trace, which is the key trick in Theorem 2.2.2.

Symmetric matrices (\(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}^\top\)) have real eigenvalues and an orthogonal eigendecomposition \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf{Q}^\top\), \(\mathbf{Q}^\top\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}\).

Idempotent matrices (\(\mathbf{A}^2 = \mathbf{A}\)). If \(\mathbf{A}\) is symmetric and idempotent, its eigenvalues are 0 or 1 and \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A})\) (Exercise B.3). Example: the centring matrix \(\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{I}_n - \tfrac1n\mathbf{J}_n\), for which \(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y} - \bar y\mathbf{1}\) and \(\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y} = S_{yy}\).

Positive (semi)definite matrices. A symmetric \(\mathbf{A}\) is positive semidefinite if \(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{a} \ge 0\) for all \(\mathbf{a}\), and positive definite if \(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{a} > 0\) for all \(\mathbf{a} \ne \mathbf{0}\). A positive definite matrix has positive eigenvalues and is invertible.

Lemma 2.2.1 (\(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\))

\(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite. It is positive definite, and hence invertible, if and only if \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p + 1\) (assumption A0).

Derivation

Symmetry: \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^\top = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\). For any \(\mathbf{a}\), \(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{a} = (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{a})^\top(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{a}) = \lVert\mathbf{X}\mathbf{a}\rVert^2 \ge 0\), with equality iff \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}\). A non-zero \(\mathbf{a}\) with \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}\) exists iff the columns of \(\mathbf{X}\) are linearly dependent, i.e. iff \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) < p + 1\).

Derivatives of linear and quadratic forms. For a vector variable \(\mathbf{b}\), define \(\partial f/\partial\mathbf{b}\) as the column vector of partial derivatives. Then \[ \frac{\partial\,(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{b})}{\partial\mathbf{b}} = \mathbf{a}, \qquad \frac{\partial\,(\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b})}{\partial\mathbf{b}} = (\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{A}^\top)\mathbf{b} = 2\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}\ \text{ if } \mathbf{A} \text{ is symmetric.} \] Both follow by writing the forms as sums and differentiating with respect to one coordinate \(b_k\) (Exercise B.1).

4.3 Random vectors

For a random vector \(\mathbf{y} = (y_1,\dots,y_n)^\top\), the mean vector is \(\boldsymbol\mu = E(\mathbf{y})\) with entries \(E(y_i)\), and the covariance matrix is \[ \boldsymbol\Sigma = \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{y}) = E\bigl[(\mathbf{y} - \boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf{y} - \boldsymbol\mu)^\top\bigr], \qquad \Sigma_{ik} = \operatorname{Cov}(y_i, y_k). \] \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite, because \(\mathbf{a}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{a} = \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{y}) \ge 0\).

Theorem 2.2.1 (Linear transformations)

For a fixed \(m\times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) and fixed \(m\times1\) vector \(\mathbf{c}\), \[ E(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf{c}, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{A}^\top . \] More generally, \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{B}^\top\).

Derivation

The mean result is linearity of expectation applied to each entry \(\sum_k a_{ik}y_k + c_i\). For the covariance, \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{c} - (\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{y} - \boldsymbol\mu)\), so \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{c}) = E[\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\mathbf{A}^\top] = \mathbf{A}\,E[(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top]\,\mathbf{A}^\top = \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{A}^\top\), since \(\mathbf{A}\) is fixed. The cross-covariance follows in the same way from \(E[\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\mathbf{B}^\top]\).

For example, if \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{y}) = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_n\) and \(\mathbf{a} = \tfrac1n\mathbf{1}\), then \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar y) = \sigma^2\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{a} = \sigma^2/n\). In Lesson 2.4 the same rule, with \(\mathbf{A} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\), gives \(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})\) in one line.

Theorem 2.2.2 (Expectation of a quadratic form)

For a fixed symmetric \(n\times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) and any random vector \(\mathbf{y}\) with mean \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and covariance \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\), \[ E(\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma) + \boldsymbol\mu^\top\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\mu . \] No normality is needed.

Derivation

Write \(\mathbf{y} = \boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf{z}\) with \(E(\mathbf{z}) = \mathbf{0}\), \(E(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^\top) = \boldsymbol\Sigma\). Then \(\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} = \boldsymbol\mu^\top\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\mu + 2\boldsymbol\mu^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} + \mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}\), and the middle term has expectation 0. The last term is a scalar, so it equals its trace: \(E(\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}) = E[\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z})] = E[\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^\top)] = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\,E(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^\top)) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma)\), using the cyclic property and the linearity of trace and expectation.

Example. With \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{C}\) (centring) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_n\): \(E(S_{yy}) = \sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}) + \boldsymbol\mu^\top\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol\mu = (n-1)\sigma^2 + \sum_i(\mu_i - \bar\mu)^2\). If all means are equal the second term vanishes and \(S_{yy}/(n-1)\) is unbiased for \(\sigma^2\); if the means differ, it is not. In regression the means differ (\(\mu_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\)), which is why \(\hat\sigma^2\) uses the residuals instead, and Lesson 2.4 shows \(E(\mathrm{SSE}) = (n-p-1)\sigma^2\) with the same theorem.

4.4 The multivariate normal distribution

Definition

\(\mathbf{y}\) has an \(n\)-variate normal distribution, \(\mathbf{y} \sim N_n(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\), if every linear combination \(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{y}\) has a (univariate) normal distribution; then \(E(\mathbf{y}) = \boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{y}) = \boldsymbol\Sigma\). If \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) is positive definite, \(\mathbf{y}\) has density \[ f(\mathbf{y}) = (2\pi)^{-n/2}\,|\boldsymbol\Sigma|^{-1/2}\exp\!\Bigl\{-\tfrac12(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)\Bigr\}. \]

Theorem 2.2.3 (Properties of the multivariate normal)

Let \(\mathbf{y} \sim N_n(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\).

  1. For fixed \(\mathbf{A}\) (\(m\times n\)) and \(\mathbf{c}\): \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{c} \sim N_m(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf{c}, \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{A}^\top)\).
  2. Every sub-vector of \(\mathbf{y}\) is multivariate normal (take \(\mathbf{A}\) to select its entries).
  3. If \(\mathbf{y}\) is partitioned into sub-vectors \(\mathbf{y}_1, \mathbf{y}_2\), then \(\mathbf{y}_1\) and \(\mathbf{y}_2\) are independent if and only if \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{y}_1, \mathbf{y}_2) = \mathbf{0}\). Consequently, \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}\) are independent iff \(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{B}^\top = \mathbf{0}\).
Derivation
  1. Any linear combination of \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{c}\) is \(\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{c} = (\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{b})^\top\mathbf{y} + \text{const}\), a linear combination of \(\mathbf{y}\) plus a constant, hence normal; the mean and covariance come from Theorem 2.2.1. (b) is (a) with a selection matrix.
  2. For positive definite \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\): if the cross-covariance is zero, \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) is block diagonal, so \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\) is block diagonal, \(|\boldsymbol\Sigma| = |\boldsymbol\Sigma_{11}||\boldsymbol\Sigma_{22}|\), and the quadratic form in the exponent splits into a term in \(\mathbf{y}_1\) plus a term in \(\mathbf{y}_2\); the density factorises. The converse holds for any distribution with finite variances. (The singular case follows via characteristic functions; see Seber and Lee, 2003, Chapter 2.) For the last statement, apply the result to the jointly normal vector \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf{A}\\ \mathbf{B}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\mathbf{y}\), whose cross-covariance block is \(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{B}^\top\).

Part (c) is special to the normal distribution: in general, uncorrelated random variables need not be independent. It is the tool that will show \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) and SSE are independent in Lesson 2.4, completing the argument deferred in Module 1.

5 Worked example

5.1 The design matrix for mpg on weight and model year

For the model of Lesson 2.1, \(p = 2\), \(n = 398\), and the first rows of \(\mathbf{X}\) (weight in 1000 lb, model year as recorded) are

Car \(y_i\) (mpg) Constant Weight Model year
chevrolet chevelle malibu 18 1 3.504 70
buick skylark 320 15 1 3.693 70
plymouth satellite 18 1 3.436 70

The entries of \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) and \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\) are sums of squares and cross-products of the columns: \[ \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix} n & \sum x_{i1} & \sum x_{i2}\\ \sum x_{i1} & \sum x_{i1}^2 & \sum x_{i1}x_{i2}\\ \sum x_{i2} & \sum x_{i1}x_{i2} & \sum x_{i2}^2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 398 & 1182.229 & 30252\\ 1182.229 & 3796.427 & 89480.187\\ 30252 & 89480.187 & 2304884 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 9358.8\\ 25614.041\\ 718009.1 \end{pmatrix}. \] \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = 3\), and the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) are \(0.749\), \(322.3\) and \(2{,}308{,}755\): all positive, so \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) is positive definite (Lemma 2.2.1). Solving the linear system \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\) reproduces the estimates of Lesson 2.1, \((-14.198, -6.664, 0.757)\); Lesson 2.3 shows why this system defines the least-squares estimator.

A numerical warning. The ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue (the condition number of \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\)) is about \(3.1\times10^6\). The cause is not a statistical problem but the scale of the columns: model year values around 76 are nearly proportional to the constant column. After centring (weight at 3000 lb, year at 1976) the condition number is \(21.3\), while the fitted values and slopes are unchanged. Software therefore never inverts \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) directly; it uses QR or Cholesky decompositions (Lesson 2.3). Module 5 returns to ill-conditioning that does have a statistical cause.

R code 
cols <- c("mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
          "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name")
auto <- read.table("data/auto-mpg.data", col.names = cols, na.strings = "?")
auto$w <- auto$weight / 1000

X <- model.matrix(~ w + model_year, data = auto)   # the design matrix
y <- auto$mpg
head(X, 3)
XtX <- crossprod(X)                                # t(X) %*% X
Xty <- crossprod(X, y)
XtX; Xty
qr(X)$rank
ev <- eigen(XtX, symmetric = TRUE)$values
ev; max(ev) / min(ev)                              # condition number
solve(XtX, Xty)                                    # same estimates as Lesson 2.1

Xc <- model.matrix(~ I(w - 3) + I(model_year - 76), data = auto)
evc <- eigen(crossprod(Xc), symmetric = TRUE)$values
max(evc) / min(evc)
Python code 
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

cols = ["mpg", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight",
        "acceleration", "model_year", "origin", "car_name"]
auto = pd.read_csv("data/auto-mpg.data", sep=r"\s+", header=None,
                   names=cols, na_values="?", quotechar='"')
y = auto["mpg"].to_numpy()
w = auto["weight"].to_numpy() / 1000
yr = auto["model_year"].to_numpy().astype(float)

X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), w, yr])       # the design matrix
print(X[:3])
XtX, Xty = X.T @ X, X.T @ y
print(XtX.round(3)); print(Xty.round(3))
print("rank:", np.linalg.matrix_rank(X))
ev = np.linalg.eigvalsh(XtX)                        # ascending order
print("eigenvalues:", ev.round(3), " condition number:", f"{ev[-1] / ev[0]:.4g}")
print("solution:", np.linalg.solve(XtX, Xty).round(4))

Xc = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), w - 3, yr - 76])
evc = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Xc.T @ Xc)
print("condition number after centring:", round(evc[-1] / evc[0], 2))

5.2 When the full-rank condition fails

Two design matrices that violate A0:

  • The same variable in two units. Columns (constant, weight in lb, weight in kg): the kg column is \(0.45359237\times\) the lb column, so \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = 2 < 3\).
  • The dummy-variable trap. Columns (constant, American, European, Japanese) with indicators for all three origins: the three indicators sum to the constant column, so \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = 3 < 4\) and \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) has a zero eigenvalue. Lesson 2.9 resolves this by dropping one indicator.
R code 
# Uses auto from Section 5.1 (run that code first).
X_units <- cbind(1, auto$weight, auto$weight * 0.45359237)
qr(X_units)$rank
X_trap <- cbind(1, sapply(1:3, function(k) as.numeric(auto$origin == k)))
qr(X_trap)$rank
round(eigen(crossprod(X_trap), symmetric = TRUE)$values, 6)
Python code 
# Uses np, y and auto from Section 5.1 (run that code first).
X_units = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), auto["weight"], auto["weight"] * 0.45359237])
print("rank with lb and kg:", np.linalg.matrix_rank(X_units))
X_trap = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y))] + [(auto["origin"] == k).astype(float) for k in (1, 2, 3)])
print("rank with all three origin indicators:", np.linalg.matrix_rank(X_trap))
print("eigenvalues:", np.linalg.eigvalsh(X_trap.T @ X_trap).round(6))

5.3 Checking Theorems 2.2.1 and 2.2.2 by simulation

Let \(n = 5\), \(\boldsymbol\mu = (1,2,3,4,5)^\top\) and \(\mathbf{y} \sim N_5(\boldsymbol\mu, 4\mathbf{I}_5)\). Theorem 2.2.2 with the centring matrix predicts \[ E(S_{yy}) = (n-1)\sigma^2 + \sum_i(\mu_i - \bar\mu)^2 = 4 \times 4 + 10 = 26, \] so \(S_{yy}/(n-1)\) has mean \(6.5\), not \(\sigma^2 = 4\), because the means differ. With \(\mathbf{A}\) forming the averages of the first two and the last three entries, Theorem 2.2.1 predicts \(E(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}) = (1.5, 4)^\top\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}) = 4\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^\top = \operatorname{diag}(2, 1.333)\). In 100,000 simulated vectors (Python, seed 2026) the average of \(S_{yy}\) is \(25.91\) (Monte Carlo standard error \(0.05\), so within two standard errors of 26), the mean of \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}\) is \((1.508, 3.995)\), and its sample covariance matrix has diagonal \((1.999, 1.332)\) and off-diagonal \(-0.007\). R uses a different random-number generator, so its simulated values differ slightly.

R code 
# Self-contained: needs nothing from earlier sections.
set.seed(2026)
n <- 5; mu <- 1:5; sigma <- 2; R <- 1e5
C <- diag(n) - matrix(1, n, n) / n
Y <- matrix(rnorm(R * n, sd = sigma), R, n) + matrix(mu, R, n, byrow = TRUE)
q <- rowSums((Y %*% C) * Y)                         # y' C y for every row
c(sim_mean = mean(q), mc_se = sd(q) / sqrt(R),
  theory = (n - 1) * sigma^2 + drop(t(mu) %*% C %*% mu))

A <- rbind(c(1, 1, 0, 0, 0) / 2, c(0, 0, 1, 1, 1) / 3)
Z <- Y %*% t(A)
colMeans(Z); drop(A %*% mu)
cov(Z); sigma^2 * A %*% t(A)
Python code 
# Self-contained: needs nothing from earlier sections.
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
n, mu, sigma, R = 5, np.arange(1.0, 6.0), 2.0, 100_000
C = np.eye(n) - np.ones((n, n)) / n
Y = mu + sigma * rng.standard_normal((R, n))
q = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Y, C, Y)               # y' C y for every row
print(f"simulated mean {q.mean():.3f} (MC se {q.std() / np.sqrt(R):.3f}); "
      f"theory {(n - 1) * sigma**2 + mu @ C @ mu:.1f}")

A = np.array([[1, 1, 0, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1, 1, 1]]) / np.array([[2], [3]])
Z = Y @ A.T
print("mean of Ay:", Z.mean(axis=0).round(3), " theory:", A @ mu)
print("cov of Ay:\n", np.cov(Z.T).round(3), "\ntheory:\n", (sigma**2 * A @ A.T).round(3))

6 Common misconceptions

Misconceptions

  1. “\(\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}\) means the errors are independent.” — It means uncorrelated with equal variances. Independence follows only with normality (Theorem 2.2.3c).
  2. “Uncorrelated random variables are independent.” — Only for jointly normal variables. (\(Z\) and \(Z^2 - 1\) with \(Z \sim N(0,1)\) are uncorrelated but dependent.)
  3. “\(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) is invertible whenever \(n > p + 1\).” — Enough observations are necessary, not sufficient: the columns must also be linearly independent.
  4. “\((\mathbf{AB})^\top = \mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{B}^\top\).” — The order reverses: \((\mathbf{AB})^\top = \mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{A}^\top\).
  5. “A huge condition number of \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) always means collinearity.” — It can be caused by the scale or location of the columns; centring and scaling remove that part (worked example).

7 Key takeaways

  • The model is \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\) with \(E(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \mathbf{0}\), \(\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_n\), and \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p + 1\).
  • \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\) is positive definite exactly when \(\mathbf{X}\) has full column rank.
  • \(E(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf{c}\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{A}^\top\).
  • \(E(\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma) + \boldsymbol\mu^\top\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\mu\), without normality.
  • Linear transformations of multivariate normal vectors are normal, and for jointly normal vectors zero covariance means independence.

8 Exercises

8.1 A. Conceptual

A.1 (★). Write the design matrix (first three rows and its dimensions) for the Auto MPG model of mpg on weight (1000 lb), weight squared and horsepower (100 hp), fitted to the 392 cars with horsepower recorded. Is this a linear model?

Solution

\(\mathbf{X}\) is \(392\times4\) with rows \((1, x_{i1}, x_{i1}^2, x_{i2})\); e.g. the first car (3504 lb, 130 hp) gives \((1, 3.504, 12.278, 1.30)\). The model is linear in the coefficients, so it is a linear model, although \(E(y)\) is a quadratic function of weight.

A.2 (★). Decide whether each design has full column rank, and explain: (a) constant, weight in lb, weight in 1000 lb; (b) constant, weight, horsepower, with \(n = 3\) observations; (c) constant and indicators for European and Japanese origin.

Solution
  1. No: the third column is the second divided by 1000. (b) Possibly: with \(n = 3\) and \(p + 1 = 3\) columns, full rank is possible if the three rows are linearly independent, but then the fit is exact, SSE = 0 and \(n - p - 1 = 0\) degrees of freedom are left for estimating \(\sigma^2\). (c) Yes, as long as each origin occurs: American cars are the reference group (Lesson 2.9).

8.2 B. Mathematical

B.1 (★★). Prove \(\partial(\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b})/\partial\mathbf{b} = (\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{A}^\top)\mathbf{b}\).

Solution

\(\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b} = \sum_i\sum_j b_i a_{ij} b_j\). Differentiating with respect to \(b_k\), the terms with \(i = k\) give \(\sum_j a_{kj}b_j\) and those with \(j = k\) give \(\sum_i b_i a_{ik}\). Hence the \(k\)-th partial derivative is \((\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b})_k + (\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{b})_k\), and the gradient is \((\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{A}^\top)\mathbf{b}\), equal to \(2\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}\) if \(\mathbf{A}\) is symmetric.

B.2 (★★). Show that the centring matrix \(\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{I}_n - \tfrac1n\mathbf{J}_n\) is symmetric and idempotent, that \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}) = n - 1\), and that \(\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y} = \sum_i(y_i - \bar y)^2\).

Solution

Symmetry is clear. \(\mathbf{J}_n^2 = \mathbf{1}(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{1})\mathbf{1}^\top = n\mathbf{J}_n\), so \(\mathbf{C}^2 = \mathbf{I} - \tfrac2n\mathbf{J} + \tfrac1{n^2}n\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{C}\). \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}) = n - \tfrac1n\cdot n = n - 1\). Finally, \(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y} - \bar y\mathbf{1}\), and \(\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{C}^\top\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y} = \lVert\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}\rVert^2 = \sum_i(y_i - \bar y)^2\).

B.3 (★★). Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be symmetric and idempotent. Show that its eigenvalues are 0 or 1, and that \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A})\).

Solution

If \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}\) with \(\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}\), then \(\lambda\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{A}^2\mathbf{v} = \lambda^2\mathbf{v}\), so \(\lambda^2 = \lambda\) and \(\lambda \in \{0, 1\}\). Write \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf{Q}^\top\) with orthogonal \(\mathbf{Q}\): \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol\Lambda)\) = number of eigenvalues equal to 1 = \(\sum_i\lambda_i = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A})\).

B.4 (★★). Let \(\mathbf{y} \sim N_n(\mu\mathbf{1}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_n)\). Using Theorem 2.2.3(c), show that \(\bar y\) and the vector of deviations \(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}\) are independent. Why does this imply that \(\bar y\) and the sample variance \(s^2\) are independent?

Solution

\(\bar y = \mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{y}\) with \(\mathbf{a} = \tfrac1n\mathbf{1}\), and \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}) = \sigma^2\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{C}^\top = \tfrac{\sigma^2}{n}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{1})^\top = \mathbf{0}^\top\), since \(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}\). Both are linear transformations of the same normal vector, so they are jointly normal and hence independent. \(s^2 = (\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y})^\top(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y})/(n-1)\) is a function of \(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}\) alone, so it is independent of \(\bar y\). Lesson 2.4 uses the same argument with \(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H}\) in place of \(\mathbf{C}\).

8.3 C. Computational

C.1 (★). For the 392 cars with horsepower recorded, build \(\mathbf{X}\) for mpg on weight (1000 lb) and horsepower (100 hp), and compute \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\), its eigenvalues and its condition number. Solve \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\) and compare with Lesson 2.1, Exercise C.2.

Solution

\(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix} 392 & 1167.213 & 409.52\\ 1167.213 & 3757.575 & 1329.899\\ 409.52 & 1329.899 & 485.752 \end{pmatrix}\), eigenvalues \(12.06\), \(29.20\) and \(4594.1\), condition number \(380.9\). The solution is \((45.640, -5.794, -4.730)\), the same as in Lesson 2.1, Exercise C.2. The condition number is larger than for the centred weight + year design (21.3); part of it is due to the uncentred columns, part to the correlation \(r = 0.865\) between weight and horsepower (compare by centring both columns).

C.2 (★★). Repeat the simulation of the worked example with all means equal, \(\boldsymbol\mu = 3\cdot\mathbf{1}_5\). What does Theorem 2.2.2 predict for \(E(S_{yy})\), and what do you observe?

Solution

With equal means, \(\boldsymbol\mu^\top\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol\mu = 0\), so \(E(S_{yy}) = (n-1)\sigma^2 = 16\) and \(s^2 = S_{yy}/4\) is unbiased for \(\sigma^2 = 4\). With seed 2026 in Python: average \(S_{yy} = 16.02\) (MC se 0.04), average \(s^2 = 4.005\).
